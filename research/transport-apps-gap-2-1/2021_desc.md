Why it matters. Neither point that touches this decision put it to Simon, because each assumed the other's answer: the grid and state-sync designs assumed the helper marshals each panel write onto the loop promptly and priced the acknowledgement at a few milliseconds, while the adapter design measured that call at about 1.1 ms at p99 on the clock and moved the crossing into a queue drained at the sequencer's own hooks at no measurable cost. Both are right about what they measured. The combination has a consequence neither states: `beat` fires once every 24 pulses (`subsequence/sequencer.py:1427`; `pulses_per_beat` is set from `MIDI_QUARTER_NOTE`, which is 24, at `subsequence/sequencer.py:357` and `subsequence/constants/__init__.py:22`) and `reschedule_pulse` once per pattern cycle (`subsequence/sequencer.py:1804`), so under the drain rule a tap waits **up to one beat** to be applied — and the `ack` that moves the cell's face leaves at application. That is 500 ms at 120 BPM, 1000 ms at 60 BPM, 333 ms at 180 BPM, against the specification's decided table, which says a step tap's transport latency is irrelevant because it is heard on the next cycle by design *and the cell should light at once*. The arithmetic is now a measurement: drained at `beat`, the wait from the link thread to the write landing on the loop had a median of 243 to 251 ms and a maximum of 497 to 500 ms at 120 BPM (#2025).

Tap to sound is unchanged in every column. The `reschedule_pulse` drain is awaited immediately before the rebuild that consumes the grid (`subsequence/sequencer.py:1804`, ahead of the rebuild loop that begins at `subsequence/sequencer.py:1808` and ends with the `pattern_reschedule` emit at `1829`), so a queued tap still makes the cycle it was going to make. What moves is the face, not the note.

The cause of the millisecond in column B is CPython's `EpollSelector` rounding every positive selector timeout up to a whole millisecond (`/usr/lib/python3.12/selectors.py:451-459`) while `BaseEventLoop._run_once` passes a timeout of zero only when a callback is already ready (`/usr/lib/python3.12/asyncio/base_events.py:1941-1949`), so a mid-sleep wake-up overshoots the clock's 1 ms spin margin (`subsequence/sequencer.py:465`, the spin at `1556-1562`). That is an engine-side lever, not a transport property, and it is #1974. It also explains why the cost is not monotonic in message rate: it peaks where wake-ups are frequent enough to catch most pulse sleeps but too sparse to keep the ready queue non-empty, which is at or near one wake-up per pulse — 48 Hz at 120 BPM, 24 Hz at 60 BPM, 72 Hz at 180 BPM — and is gone by 200 Hz.

Why C is a real column. The engine already writes `composition.data` from a non-asyncio thread: the rtmidi callback applies CC input mappings inline on its own thread, on the stated ground that "Single dict writes are safe from a non-asyncio thread under CPython's GIL" (`subsequence/sequencer.py:770-772`, the write at `803`), and the Supervisor lesson the specification carries is atomic reference swaps rather than locks across thread boundaries. #1914's state-location table records the hazard and the fix: growing a nested dict while the builder iterates it raises `RuntimeError`, and "whole-grid replacement avoids the iteration race by construction". What C costs is #1925's stated invariant, that every write reaching the grid is applied on the loop and therefore has a total order without any clock; off the loop there is no order against the algorithm's writes inside the rebuild, and the version counter, the equality test and the ordered outbound log all move off the thread that owns the current value. Taking C means re-opening #1925's version rules.

Three readings, all now measured over a real socket rather than inferred:

- **For an isolated tap, waking the loop costs a handful of late pulses, not none.** Over a socket at 5 Hz the per-message crossing left 4 to 7 pulses over 1 ms in 1536 at p99 0.55 to 0.73 ms. The earlier in-process figure of p99 0.168 ms and no late pulse came from a producer thread with no socket read, no frame assembly and no JSON parse, and is the wrong price for an adapter. Either way it is far below the paint-gesture rows and far below anything a listener can hear against a clock whose own mean jitter is about 3 us.
- **The rate the crossing rule exists for is the paint gesture, and that is where it costs most.** A held paint along a row is 16 to 32 `set` frames a second (#1971, #1941), and 25 Hz over a socket cost 26 to 48 pulses over 1 ms in 1536. Four times that traffic costs nothing at all.
- **Neither coalescing device fires at a rate a hand produces.** Scheduling a drain only when none is pending left the wake-up count equal to the message count at 5, 25, 50 and 200 Hz, in every block that ran the row, with a largest batch of one; at 500 Hz it saved seven wake-ups in 14087. Batching at the socket read behaves the same way at those rates — 170 wake-ups for 170 messages at 5 Hz, 838 for 838 at 25 Hz, and six saved in 14218 at 500 Hz — and is not a defect in either: any device can only merge frames that arrive closer together than the loop takes to service one, and any window wide enough to merge frames 40 ms apart is itself 40 ms of added latency. What separates them is a burst, where the flag catches about eight frames and the read takes the whole burst.

Read-batching takes one crossing per burst exactly on a quiet machine, at both burst sizes, and does nothing whatever at 5 Hz or at 500 Hz — which is the point: it acts only where frames are actually queued behind one another. A runaway client at 500 Hz costs the clock nothing under any device, which is why the ceiling below is a cut and not a throttle.

Both engine-side levers (#1974) remove the late pulses and neither is free where it would be applied: widening the spin margin from 1 ms to 2 ms gave p99 0.14 to 0.34 ms with no late pulse at 25 and 50 Hz and roughly doubles the clock's CPU (0.44 to 0.45 s of user CPU over four bars at the default margin, 0.67 s at 2 ms, 1.40 s at 4 ms), and it has no public setter — the only public control is `disable_spin_wait()` (`subsequence/sequencer.py:602`), which turns spin off rather than widening it. A `SelectSelector` loop gave p99 0.002 to 0.022 ms with no late pulse, but the selector cannot be confined to one loop by a policy, because `asyncio.set_event_loop_policy` is process-wide and `composition.play()` runs the clock with `asyncio.run(self._run())` (`subsequence/composition.py:5421`) with no way to pass a loop in.

**Recommended: B, with batching at the socket read as the adapter's inbound rule.** Cross onto the loop as each grid set arrives, and let the link thread take every frame the connection can hand over without waiting on the network before it crosses. The reason is the specification's own decided table: a step tap's transport latency is irrelevant because it is heard on the next cycle by design, *and the cell should light at once*. Under #1925's recommended pending look the face is the server's value, so the `ack` is the face, and a face a measured quarter of a second behind the finger — half a second at the tail — fails that requirement outright. What B costs instead is 26 to 48 pulses in 1536 arriving about a millisecond late during a paint gesture, against a clock whose mean jitter is about 3 us and a rebuild that already costs about 105 us of the same loop, and nothing at all when the panel is not being painted.

Five things changed in this recommendation on 2026-09-03, four of them from #2025 and one measured for this revision:

- **The pending flag is dropped and batching at the socket read takes its place.** The flag never fires below 500 Hz, adds about 0.05 ms to every crossing for the queue hop, and on a burst catches about nine frames rather than the burst. Read-batching costs the same nothing at a hand rate and takes a whole burst in one crossing. It is not free either: because the link thread keeps reading before it crosses, a frame early in a burst waits for the rest of it, so on bursts of 128 the crossing median was 1.29 ms with a p99 of 12.6 ms and a maximum of 15.8 ms, against a median of 0.07 ms and a p99 of 1.10 ms for the flag on the same traffic, and between 0.03 and 0.23 ms more than per-message marshalling across the hand-rate rows measured. That is the right trade: it lengthens the crossing only where the traffic is bulk and no cell is waiting on an `ack` a finger can feel, and it is two orders of magnitude inside the beat the drain rule costs.
- **The probe must not cancel the read.** `asyncio.wait_for(ws.recv(), 0)` is the natural way to ask for a frame if one is ready and it never batches anything, because CPython wraps the coroutine in a task, finds it not done and cancels it (`/usr/lib/python3.12/asyncio/tasks.py:472-517`); measured over a real link at bursts of 16, 128 and 1024 it gave a largest batch of one every time. One long-lived `recv` task probed with `asyncio.wait({task}, timeout=0)`, which never cancels (`/usr/lib/python3.12/asyncio/tasks.py:521-561`), took the whole burst at all three sizes.
- **The bulk fallback to A is dropped, not re-grounded.** It was justified on the clock and the clock refutes it: a 1024-frame burst handed straight to B costs zero late pulses and a 128-frame burst one, while the dearest traffic of all is the human's tap the fallback exempts. The remaining candidate ground, that a bulk write needs no per-cell `ack` and A gives the drain a natural batch, does not survive either: read-batching applies the same whole burst in one drain, so A buys no larger batch than B now does. And the traffic the fallback names does not exist inbound — a page-load seed travels outbound as `snapshot` from the app and again from the service (#1920, #1916), and an algorithm's bulk write never leaves the process; the only genuine inbound bulk is a panel-side bulk edit (clear, fill, paste, load a preset), which read-batching already collapses. If a bulk edit's `ack` traffic is the worry, the fix is a batched `set` frame with one `seq` and one `ack`, which is a contract question and is filed separately.
- **The rate ceiling is recast as a safety valve, and it must cut rather than throttle.** A ceiling that caught a held paint gesture would have to sit at or below the gesture's own 16 to 32 frames a second, so it would either never fire or hand the whole stroke to A and put the face a beat behind the finger for the length of the stroke. Above the gesture there is nothing to catch: a client stuck at 500 Hz, twenty times the gesture, left no pulse over 1 ms in 1536 at p99 0.455 ms per message and 0.525 ms batched, and 200 Hz was the same. Worse, the cost peaks at about one wake-up per pulse, so a limiter that smooths a runaway client down to a fixed rate in the tens of hertz parks it on the worst point of the curve, and because that peak moves with tempo no fixed rate is safe at every tempo. The valve is therefore the bounded queue #1928 already specifies, which drops and counts when full, plus a `nack` and a close on sustained overrun — never a token bucket clamping to tens of hertz.
- **The 5 Hz row is re-cited from the socket figure.** A lone tap does cost 4 to 7 late pulses in 1536, not none.

#1974 stays the lever if the cost on the real headless server is worse than the workstation's, and C stays the escape that buys A's clock cost with B's face at the price of re-opening #1925. Where B's few milliseconds come from: #1925 prices them from #1920's measurement, a 0.8 to 1 ms loopback browser round-trip plus about 1 ms of wire, and the marshal onto the loop is now measured at 0.05 to 0.30 ms rather than assumed to be a millisecond, so the few milliseconds are all network; the end-to-end figure across a real LAN is #2002 and is unmeasured, so the budget is an estimate with two measured terms.

**Answer this with #1968.** The pairings: B (or C) with the ring, where nothing on a face is ever speculative and the face follows within milliseconds; A with the optimistic face, where the cell flips locally on the finger and the server's value corrects it up to a beat later, which is what makes A survivable; and A with an `ack` sent by the service on receipt rather than at engine application, where the ring clears in milliseconds against a value the service has accepted and every panel is told, so panel agreement survives and the clock is untouched — at the price of a second version axis beside #1925's applied version, and of an `ack` that no longer means what #1925 defines it to mean. A with the ring as #1925 defines it is the combination to avoid, and it is the combination the points as filed would have produced. #1968's own body says the two looks are "both inside 0.1 s on the LAN", which holds only under B or C.

Two choices follow from this answer and are filed separately rather than decided here: whether #1928's inbound rule is rewritten to read-batching-and-cross (#2030), and whether the envelope gains a batched `set` frame now that it is not needed for the wake-up (#2031).
