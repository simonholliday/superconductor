**Question.** What does #2021's crossing recommendation become once #2025's measurements are carried into it, and what does that leave open?

## Where the documents stood

#2021 is the in-force spike for the one open coupling #2018 names: whether a panel grid set wakes the composition loop for a few-millisecond acknowledgement or waits for the next `reschedule_pulse` or `beat` drain. It is `needs_input`, it is the item Simon actually answers, and until this revision it recommended "**B, with A retained as the automatic fallback** for anything that is not a human's tap — a page-load seed, an algorithm's bulk write, a held paint gesture beyond a rate ceiling". #2025 measured both halves of that fallback and both fail:

- **Bulk is the cheapest traffic there is.** 8192 `set` frames delivered as eight bursts of 1024 cost zero pulses over 1 ms in 1536 and a p99 of 0.034 ms, and a 128-frame burst cost one late pulse, against 26 to 48 late pulses for a tenth of that traffic spread evenly at the paint rate of 25 Hz. The dearest traffic on the clock is the human's tap, which is the one case the fallback exempts.
- **The rate ceiling has nowhere to sit.** A ceiling that caught a held paint gesture would have to sit at or below the gesture's own 16 to 32 frames a second, so it would either never fire or hand the whole stroke to A and put the face a beat behind the finger for the length of the stroke; above the gesture there is nothing left to catch, because 200 and 500 Hz cost no late pulse at all.

#2025 also re-priced one row of #2021's own table. Its "Measured here, isolated taps at 5 Hz" figures for column B — p99 0.168 ms, maximum 0.351 ms, no pulse over 1 ms in 1536 — came from an in-process producer thread calling `call_soon_threadsafe` directly, with no socket read, no frame assembly and no JSON parse. Over a real `websockets` link the same rate costs 4 to 7 late pulses at p99 0.55 to 0.73 ms, which this point reproduced independently at 6 late pulses and p99 0.613 ms on its own quiet block.

Two further things were true of the documents and are the reason this is a design rather than an edit. #2025 leaves five choices in its body and on no task, against the specification's rule that a question for Simon goes on a `needs_input` task: whether the pending flag is replaced by batching at the socket read, whether the bulk fallback is dropped or re-grounded, whether the rate ceiling becomes something else, whether the envelope gains a batched `set`, and whether an engine-side lever is taken. The first three are answered inside #2021's recommendation by this revision, because they are what that spike is for; the fourth and the part of the first that rewrites #1928's rule were on no spike at all and are #2030 and #2031 now; and #1974 already carried the engine-lever question and needed only a link, which it has.

## What was measured here

The instrument is #2025's, which is `benchmarks/clock_jitter.py`'s: the real `subsequence.sequencer.Sequencer` with `_jitter_log` set, 16 bars at 120 BPM (1536 pulses of 20.833 ms), spin-wait on, no MIDI port opened. One adapter shape per run inside the sequencer process, a separate sender process as the stand-in service, each inbound frame becoming one dict write on the clock loop. Machine: the development workstation (Intel i7-9700K, 8 cores, Ubuntu 6.8.0-138, Python 3.12.3, `websockets` 17.1), single runs, load-gated, indicative only.

Two blocks were run. The first was discarded: another research point was running the same bench from the same shared scratchpad copy on the same ports at the same time, and one row came back with a hundred and twenty-eight-frame label and sixteen-frame contents, which is that point's traffic and not this one's. Nothing from it is quoted. Every row applied every frame it received and dropped none. "Crossing" is the interval from the frame leaving the link thread to the write landing on the clock loop; the return leg is not instrumented.

Three rows of that block matter beyond confirming #2025.

**Read-batching does not coalesce a hand's taps, and cannot.** At an isolated 5 Hz tap rate it took 170 wake-ups for 170 messages with a largest batch of one, which is exactly what per-message marshalling does, and cost 7 pulses over 1 ms in 1536 against per-message's 6 in the same block; at 500 Hz it saved six wake-ups in 14218. Neither adapter-side device buys anything at the rate the crossing rule exists for. This is not a property of an implementation: any device can only merge frames that arrive closer together than the loop takes to service one, and any window wide enough to merge frames 40 ms apart is itself 40 ms of added latency, which is the thing prompt crossing exists to avoid.

**Read-batching takes a whole burst, on a quiet machine as well as a loaded one.** 2176 messages arriving as bursts of 128 crossed 17 times, and 8192 messages arriving as bursts of 1024 crossed 8 times — one crossing per burst, with the mean and the maximum batch both equal to the burst — against 241 crossings for the same 128-frame traffic under the pending flag, whose typical batch was nine, and one crossing per message without any device. Neither burst left a pulse over 1 ms. The price is on the crossing and falls inside the burst: a 128-frame edit crossed at a median of 1.29 ms with a p99 of 12.6 ms, and a 1024-frame edit at a median of 9.40 ms with a p99 of 20.0 ms, because a frame early in a burst waits for the rest of it. Both are two orders of magnitude inside the beat the drain rule costs, and no cell in a bulk edit has a finger resting on it. #2025's read-batching burst rows were taken as counts only on a machine at load 8.7 to 9.0, because the block that would have taken them on a quiet machine was stopped when the workstation filled up; these are those rows, with their jitter and their crossing latency.

**A runaway client is cheap for the clock and dear for nothing that matters.** A client stuck at 500 Hz — twenty times the paint gesture, and above anything the browser's frame-aligned continuous input can produce — left no pulse over 1 ms in 1536 under either device, at p99 0.455 ms per message and 0.525 ms batched, against 26 to 48 late pulses at 25 Hz. Read-batching neither helps there nor needs to: a steady stream has nothing buffered to batch. So the thing a rate limiter would protect is not the clock, which is the whole of the argument below.

## The implementation trap, measured

A read-batching adapter must probe for a further frame without cancelling the read it is probing, and the natural probe silently never batches. `asyncio.wait_for(ws.recv(), 0)` wraps the coroutine in a task, finds it not done — because it has not started — and cancels it, then raises `TimeoutError` (`/usr/lib/python3.12/asyncio/tasks.py:472-517`, the `timeout is not None and timeout <= 0` branch). One long-lived `recv` task probed with `asyncio.wait({task}, timeout=0)` never cancels anything: `_wait` adds a done-callback, waits on a zero-delay timer, and on return removes the callbacks and partitions the futures into done and pending without touching the pending ones (`/usr/lib/python3.12/asyncio/tasks.py:521-561`). Measured over a real `websockets` link on localhost, four bursts of each size, the two shapes are not close:

| Probe | 16-frame bursts | 128-frame bursts | 1024-frame bursts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `asyncio.wait_for(ws.recv(), 0)` | 64 crossings for 64 frames, largest batch 1 | 512 for 512, largest batch 1 | 4096 for 4096, largest batch 1 |
| One long-lived `recv` task, `asyncio.wait({task}, timeout=0)` | 4 crossings for 64 frames, largest batch 16 | 4 for 512, largest batch 128 | 4 for 4096, largest batch 1024 |
| One long-lived `recv` task, three passes of `asyncio.sleep(0)` (#2025's shape) | 4 for 64, largest batch 16 | 4 for 512, largest batch 128 | 4 for 4096, largest batch 1024 |

`websockets` documents that "Canceling `recv` is safe. There's no risk of losing data. The next invocation of `recv` will return the next message", so the trap is not lost frames: it is a device that quietly does nothing, in a shape a reviewer would pass. The batches here are larger than the socket bench's because sender and reader share one process, so the reader cannot empty the socket mid-burst; over a real link a 1024-frame burst took three or four crossings in #2025's loaded block, and exactly one per burst in this one.

## The three consequences, and why each is what it is

**The pending flag is replaced by batching at the socket read.** The flag fires only on frames arriving back to back, and then at about eight to one, not one per burst; at every rate below 500 Hz it schedules exactly one drain per message and adds about 0.05 ms to each crossing for the queue hop. Read-batching costs the same nothing at a hand rate and takes the whole burst. Its own price is crossing latency on a burst, not on a tap: because the link thread keeps reading before it crosses, a frame early in a burst waits for the rest of it. That is the right shape — it lengthens the crossing exactly where the traffic is bulk and no cell is waiting on an `ack` a finger can feel, and leaves a lone tap crossing at once.

**The bulk fallback to A is dropped rather than re-grounded.** Its clock justification is refuted above. The only other ground available is that a bulk write needs no per-cell `ack` and that A gives the drain a natural batch, and that does not survive either, for three reasons. Read-batching applies the same whole burst in one drain, so A offers no larger batch than B now does. The `ack` count is unchanged by the choice: #1925 makes the helper append one `ack` and one `changed` per applied write to a single ordered log at the moment of application, which the link thread drains, so N cells produce N acknowledgements under A and under B alike; the only way to batch acknowledgements is to batch the request, which is #2031 and not this decision. And the traffic the fallback names is largely not inbound at all: on connect the app sends `manifest` and `snapshot` outbound and the service relays a `snapshot` to the browser (#1920, #1916), so a page-load seed never travels as inbound `set` frames, and an algorithm's bulk write never leaves the process. What remains is a panel-side bulk edit — clear a row, fill it, paste, load a preset — which read-batching already collapses into one crossing.

**The rate ceiling becomes a safety valve that cuts rather than throttles.** The clock cost is not monotonic in message rate. It peaks where wake-ups are frequent enough to catch most pulse sleeps but too sparse to keep the loop's ready queue non-empty, which is at or near one wake-up per pulse — 48 Hz at 120 BPM, 24 Hz at 60 BPM, 72 Hz at 180 BPM — and is gone by 200 Hz, because `EpollSelector.select` rounds a positive timeout up to a whole millisecond and leaves a zero timeout alone (`/usr/lib/python3.12/selectors.py:451-459`) while `BaseEventLoop._run_once` passes zero whenever a callback is already ready (`/usr/lib/python3.12/asyncio/base_events.py:1941-1949`), and the clock's spin margin is 1 ms (`subsequence/sequencer.py:465`, the spin at `1556-1562`). The consequence for a limiter is direct and is the reason this is not a footnote: **a token bucket that smooths a runaway client down to a fixed rate in the tens of hertz parks it on the worst point of the curve**, and because the peak moves with tempo no fixed rate is safe at every tempo a composition may run at. So the valve is the bounded queue #1928 already specifies, which drops and counts when full, plus a `nack` and a close on sustained overrun — a cut, never a clamp. Nothing throttles by rate anywhere in the adapter.

A fourth candidate was excluded rather than short-listed: **a timed batching window**, where the link thread waits a fixed few milliseconds before crossing so that a hand's taps merge. It is excluded because the measurements show there is nothing to merge — frames at 25 Hz are 40 ms apart and the loop services a crossing in about 0.2 ms — so a window that merged anything a hand produced would have to be tens of milliseconds wide, and would then add exactly that to the tap-to-face path the whole column exists to shorten. It is the only shape that trades latency for wake-ups, and the wake-ups are not worth buying.

## Short list

| Option | For | Against |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Batch at the socket read.** The link thread takes the frame that woke it, keeps taking frames for as long as the connection can hand one over without waiting on the network, then crosses once with a bounded drain | one crossing per burst, measured; identical to per-message at every rate a hand or a browser produces, so nothing is paid for the common case; no timer and no added delay for a lone tap; removes the last reason to keep a bulk fallback, and with it the rate ceiling; needs no change to the contract | about 0.23 ms more crossing latency on a lone frame; a burst's early frames wait for the rest of it, a 12.6 ms p99 at 128 frames and a 20.0 ms p99 at 1024; the probe must be written so that it does not cancel the read, which is the one place this can be got wrong invisibly |
| **2. Cross per message, no batching device.** One `call_soon_threadsafe` per frame | the simplest possible adapter; the lowest crossing latency for a lone frame; measured to cost the clock nothing on a burst anyway, because a burst lands inside one or two pulses | 128 self-pipe writes and 128 loop callbacks for one "clear row", where one would do; on a slower machine, where the drain takes longer, the wake-up count is the term that grows |
| **3. Keep the pending flag.** Schedule a drain only when one is not already pending | already written into #2018's table, so nothing moves | measured to fire at no rate below 500 Hz, and then to save seven wake-ups in 14087; on a burst it catches about eight or nine frames, not the burst; it adds a lock, a flag and about 0.05 ms to every crossing in exchange; it is strictly dominated by both neighbours |

**Recommended: 1, batching at the socket read, with a bounded drain and no rate limiter.** It is the only option that delivers what #2018's crossing table claimed for the flag — one wake-up per burst — and it delivers it in the adapter, which is the cheaper of the two places to fix it, leaving the contract alone. It costs nothing at the rates the crossing rule exists for, because at those rates it does exactly what option 2 does. Its price is bounded and falls where nothing is waiting on it. And it is what lets the fallback and the ceiling go, which is worth more than the wake-ups: two conditional paths disappear from the adapter, and with them the case where a held gesture silently switches the face from milliseconds to a beat.

Two rules belong with the recommendation. **The drain is bounded**: apply at most a few score cells per callback and continue with `call_soon`, which appends to the loop's ready queue without writing the self-pipe byte (`/usr/lib/python3.12/asyncio/base_events.py:785`, `814-818`, against `call_soon_threadsafe` at `838-846` whose `_write_to_self` sends the byte at `/usr/lib/python3.12/asyncio/selector_events.py:141-153`), so a continuation costs no wake-up. At 1024 dict writes the bench saw no late pulse, so this is a guard for the day the apply is a real `StepGrid.apply()` rather than one dict write (#2009), not a measured need. **The bounded queue stays as #1928 specifies it**, dropping and counting when full, and is the whole of the overload story together with a `nack` and a close.

## What this changes elsewhere

- **#2021** is revised in place: its table gains a crossing-latency row and a paint-gesture row, its 5 Hz row is re-cited from the socket figure, its burst row carries the read-batching measurement, and its recommendation becomes B with batching at the socket read, the fallback dropped, the ceiling recast, #1974 still the lever and C still the escape. Its pairing with #1968 is unchanged.
- **#1928** states Subsequence's inbound rule as a queue drained at `reschedule_pulse` and `beat`. That is #2021's column A. If Simon answers B, that sentence has to be rewritten, and to what is #2030.
- **#1916 and #1920** carry one cell per `set`. Whether they gain a batched write is #2031. Nothing in this design needs it.
- **#1971** says painting along a row "costs nothing on the wire (one set per cell)". True of the wire; not true of the clock under B, where it is the single dearest traffic measured — 26 to 48 late pulses in 1536. That does not argue against admitting the gesture, since the late pulses are about a millisecond each against a 20.833 ms pulse, but the sentence should not be read as "costs nothing".
- **#2008** gains one column from #2025: the messages-per-wake-up count at 25 Hz on the headless server, because a slower machine takes longer to drain and could make batching begin to fire where it does not here.

## Not covered

- **Whether Simon wants B at all.** This design revises the recommendation inside #2021; the answer is his and the spike stays `needs_input`.
- **The return leg of the crossing.** Only link thread to loop is timed here and in #2025. The loop handing an `ack` back to the link thread and out of the socket is not instrumented; the end-to-end figure is #2002.
- **A real `StepGrid.apply()` in place of the dict write.** The apply step is one dict write in every row quoted. #2008 and #2009 carry that.
- **Read-batching under either engine-side lever.** Not run; there is no reason to expect a surprise, since the lever acts on the wake-up and read-batching changes only how many there are.
- **Tempi other than 120 BPM.** The pulse is 20.833 ms in every row. The peak rate scales with the pulse interval, so the ceiling argument is stated for the range 60 to 180 BPM from arithmetic, not measurement.
- **Where between 50 and 200 Hz the cost peaks.** No row sits between; nothing here depends on the shape of that curve.

## Hardware-gated

Nothing in this design needs the iiyama panel or a graphical host: it is entirely server-side. The figures are the development workstation's and are indicative; re-taking them on the headless server with whichever rule is chosen is #2008, and the one column to add there is messages per wake-up at 25 Hz. The epoll rounding is a CPython property and will be present on the server; what the server changes is how long a wake-up takes to service, and therefore the rate at which any batching device begins to fire.
