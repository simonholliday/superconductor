Summary: The ten rows #2025's block F never reached and the three 1024-frame rows its block E ran by hand were re-taken over a real `websockets` link on the workstation under a logged load gate, in four blocks with eight clean no-adapter baselines. Read-batching does collapse a burst into one crossing on a socket, exactly as #2021's producer-thread model predicted and as #2033's block H had already measured: seventeen crossings for seventeen bursts of 128 and eight for eight bursts of 1024, mean and largest batch the whole burst. The jitter these rows add does not decide the device either way, because on the clock every shape is a tie. They are consistent with the rule now in force — cross per message, no batching device — and they close the rows #2025 named under Not covered. **Question.** Over a real `websockets` link on a quiet machine, what do the ten rows #2025's block F never reached and the three 1024-frame rows its block E ran by hand actually measure, and does the jitter they carry bear on the read-batching rule #2025 recommended?

## What these rows add, and what they repeat

The gap this point was opened to close was named in #2025's own Not covered: block F stopped after ten of its twenty rows when the workstation's load rose above 8, so read-batching and the off-loop shape on bursts were left with wake-up counts taken under load and no jitter figures at all, and the flagged shape at 50 Hz under each engine-side lever was never run. Block E's three 1024-frame rows were run by hand with no entry in the runner's log, so their conditions rest on the `loadavg` fields of the rows themselves.

Part of that gap had already been closed elsewhere, and saying which part is the honest frame for everything below. **#2033's block H already carries read-batching over a real `websockets` link with full jitter and crossing columns at both burst sizes**, taken on 2026-09-03 under a gate of 1.10 at loads between 0.34 and 0.77 — lower than every comparable row here: 128 frames at 17 wake-ups for 2176 messages, p99 0.076 ms, a 0.924 ms worst pulse, no pulse over 1 ms, crossing median 1.291 ms and crossing p99 12.62 ms; 1024 frames at 8 wake-ups for 8192, p99 0.017 ms, a 0.568 ms worst pulse, no late pulse, crossing median 9.40 ms and crossing p99 20.02 ms. So the jitter of read-batching over a socket is not new here and must not be presented as new.

What these blocks genuinely add is narrower and is still worth having:

- **A second and a third independent pass of read-batching** at both burst sizes, in a different point's copy of the bench on different ports, against this block's own baselines. The 128-frame passes disagree with block H by one late pulse in 1536, which is the size of the whole effect anyone is arguing about.
- **Per-message crossing at a 128-frame burst inside the block**, which is the comparator every 128-frame claim in this research is read against and which no block of this point had taken. It is the row block F reached and spoiled.
- **Per-message crossing and the `beat` drain at 1024 frames under a logged gate**, which block E ran by hand.
- **The off-loop shape at a 1024-frame burst**, which no block anywhere had run.
- **The flagged shape at 50 Hz under each engine-side lever, and beside them an unlevered control** in the same script copy, which block F named and did not reach.
- **A crossing stopwatch on every one of those rows**, which is what makes the cost side of the trade a measurement rather than an inference.
- **A read-span probe** separating the prototype's per-frame read pattern from the batching device it is meant to measure.

## Method, and the machine it ran on

The instrument is #2025's, unchanged, which is `benchmarks/clock_jitter.py`'s: the real `subsequence.sequencer.Sequencer` constructed with `_jitter_log` set and `output_device_name="NO-SUCH-DEVICE-XYZ"`, a name matching no port, so `midi_utils` logs the miss and no MIDI device is opened; sixteen bars at 120 BPM, which is 1536 pulses of 20.833 ms; spin-wait on at the engine's own 1 ms margin (`subsequence/sequencer.py:465`, the sleep-then-busy-wait at `1556-1562`, the jitter appended at `1567`); asyncio's default epoll selector unless a row says `select`. One adapter shape per run inside the sequencer process, a separate sender process as the stand-in service, each inbound frame becoming one dict write applied on the clock loop — except in the off-loop shape, where the link thread writes it itself.

The scripts are copies of the block-F runner's, taken so this block could not collide with another point running the shared copies on the shared ports: the four port constants move to 9161, 9163, 9164 and 9165 for blocks L, M and N, and again to 9171, 9173, 9174 and 9175 for block P.

**The load gate, and what it is and is not evidence of.** The runner reads `/proc/loadavg` before every row and admits the row when the one-minute average is at or below the gate; it stamps the load on the row at its start and again at its end. Blocks L, M and N ran at a gate of 1.50 rather than the 1.20 the block-F runner used (`run_coalesce_2.sh`, `QUIET=${QUIET:-120}`). Block P, which supplies the two control rows the others lacked, ran at 1.20, so its rows are admitted on the same condition as the block-F rows they replace. Three things about the raised gate have to be said plainly, because the first draft of this finding said none of them.

- **The reason attributed to #2033's block K was not that block's reason.** #2033 says block K's last four rows "ran under a gate raised from 1.10 to 1.50, because this session's own work kept the one-minute load average above 1.10 and the original gate could not clear". Its gate was 1.10, and the cause was that session's own work. The 1.20 figure belongs to #2025's block F.
- **The stated justification for raising the gate is not attested by any log.** The runner's comment and the first draft both say another session held the one-minute average between 1.27 and 1.43 throughout. Nothing under the scratchpad records that, and the blocks' own stamps contradict "throughout": of the twenty-two row starts across blocks L, M and N, twelve were stamped at or below 1.20 — 0.61, 0.65, 0.68, 0.70, 0.75, 0.87, 0.95, 0.99, 1.03, 1.07, 1.14 and 1.16 — several of them far below it. At those moments a 1.20 gate would have admitted the row too. What is true is that the raised gate admitted the rows stamped 1.22, 1.32, 1.36, 1.37, 1.45, 1.46, 1.46, 1.48, 1.48 and 1.49, which a 1.20 gate would have made wait, and that block P later cleared a 1.20 gate on the same machine within twelve minutes of being launched at a one-minute average of 8.13.
- **The baselines do not certify the raised gate, and cannot.** Baseline jitter has no visible relationship with load anywhere in this range: the cleanest of the eight baselines, at p99 0.0011 ms and a 0.0167 ms worst pulse, is the one taken at the highest load of 1.46, and the worst, at p99 0.0167 ms and a 0.0587 ms worst pulse, is the one taken at 0.65. A control with no sensitivity to the variable it controls certifies nothing. What the baselines do say is narrower and still useful: nothing in the process at any load reached in these blocks ever produced a pulse over 1 ms, so a late pulse in an adapter row is the adapter's, not the machine's idle noise.

Two further honesties about the gate. It is evaluated only at a row's start, so a row admitted quiet can finish loaded: the `beat`-drained row in block L was admitted at 1.49 and ended at 1.52, above the gate itself. And the pending-flag row at 1024 frames in block M rose from 0.87 to 1.48 while it ran — the same rising-load signature used below to discard block L's read-batching row — and it is the row carrying this point's only 1024-frame late pulse and its 1.9092 ms worst pulse. That row is quoted with that caveat attached and nothing is built on it.

Machine: the development workstation, Intel i7-9700K, 8 cores, Ubuntu 6.8.0-138, Python 3.12.3, `websockets` 17.1. Single runs, indicative only. Every adapter row applied every frame it received and dropped none, and every adapter row pushed 69 outbound frames, one per beat, whatever the inbound traffic; the eight baseline rows received nothing and pushed nothing.

Four blocks were run. **Block L** is the ten rows block F did not reach, in block F's own order, with an opening baseline added so the burst rows have a paired no-adapter baseline at each end. **Block M** re-takes block E's three 1024-frame rows — per message, the pending flag and the `beat` drain — under the logged gate, adds a second pass of read-batching at both burst sizes, and adds the off-loop shape at 1024. **Block N** re-takes the one row of block L the load spoiled. **Block P** takes the two control rows the first draft of this finding was found to be missing: per-message crossing at a 128-frame burst, and the flagged shape at 50 Hz under no lever.

**One row is discarded and is on the record rather than dropped.** Block L's read-batching row at 1024 frames ran as another session on the workstation started heavy work: its own load stamps are 1.22 at the start and 3.70 at the end, and it came back with 19 wake-ups instead of 8, a mean batch of 431 instead of 1024, and 160 pulses over 1 ms where the same shape on a quiet machine leaves none. Nothing from it is quoted; it stays in `results/lostgap.jsonl`, and block N re-takes it.

## What the numbers mean for the device

**The wake-up arithmetic is confirmed over a socket, and it was never the doubtful part.** The figure the gap named — 89 crossings for 712 messages from #2021's in-process producer — is reproduced in kind on a real link and on a quiet machine: seventeen crossings for seventeen bursts of 128, eight for eight bursts of 1024, with a mean and a largest batch equal to the whole burst in every pass. #2033's block H had already shown the same counts on a quieter machine, so this is a second and a third confirmation rather than a first. The device does what it claims.

**On the clock, every shape is a tie, and the tie is now measured inside one block.** Read across every quiet burst row now taken by this point, by #2025's blocks A, B and E and by #2033's blocks H and K, the count of pulses over 1 ms in 1536 is zero or one whatever the shape and whatever the burst size — three in the one block-K row that carries injected tap traffic as well — and the worst single pulse in any of them is between 0.71 and 1.42 ms, at most a fifteenth of a 20.833 ms pulse.

- At 128 frames, block P's per-message row, taken at a load of 0.67 to 0.57 between two clean baselines, leaves **one** pulse over 1 ms at a 1.0302 ms worst pulse. Read-batching leaves one in both passes here and none in block H or block K. The pending flag leaves one here, one in #2025's block A and none in its block B. Per-message crossing left one in #2025's block A and one in its block B, and three in #2033's block K with an injected second-panel tap. Every paired baseline leaves none.
- At 1024 frames, read-batching leaves none in both passes here and none in block H; per-message crossing leaves none in both of its passes here and none in #2025's block E; the `beat` drain leaves none in both passes; the off-loop shape leaves none. The pending flag leaves none in one pass and one in the other, on the row whose load rose while it ran.

**That tie is a low-powered null and should be read as one.** Seventeen bursts and eight bursts, single runs per row, and a difference of one late pulse in 1536 is one arrival landing badly. The cost side of the trade is measured on 2176 and 8192 crossings apiece and is not comparable in strength: it would be wrong to say "the device buys nothing on the clock" with the confidence the crossing distributions support. What the rows support is weaker and sufficient: **no shape is distinguishable from another on the clock at either burst size, and the whole range across every block and every point is zero to one late pulse.**

**What #2025 actually argued, and what these rows do to it.** #2025 did not recommend read-batching on clock grounds. Its burst section ends "So read-batching is worth taking for the wake-ups it saves and for the socket work it avoids, **not because the clock needs it**", and it says in the same section that "a bulk burst is the cheapest traffic there is, batched or not" — which is the fact this finding measures again. Its correction cell reads "Replace the flag with batching at the socket read, which costs the same nothing at a hand rate (1.000 messages per wake-up at 25 Hz) and takes a whole burst in one crossing." So a jitter null does not refute #2025's ground. Of the two grounds #2025 gave, these rows confirm the first at both burst sizes and do not measure the second at all: nothing here counts socket syscalls, frame assemblies or JSON parses on the link thread, which is where the socket-work saving would show. #2025 had also already priced read-batching's crossing on a loaded machine, at a median of 1.21 ms at 128 frames and 6.36 ms at 1024, and argued the cost acceptable because "it lengthens the crossing exactly where the traffic is bulk and no cell is waiting on an `ack` a finger can feel". These rows put a quiet-machine figure on that crossing; they do not touch that argument, because the thing that would touch it is a cell waiting behind the batch, which is #2033's block K and not this block.

**What the device costs on these rows is the crossing, and it scales with the burst.** A frame early in a burst waits for the rest of it: the crossing median is 1.27 to 1.39 ms at 128 frames and 9.23 to 9.41 ms at 1024, with a p99 of 4.63 to 12.64 ms and 18.42 to 19.38 ms respectively. Per-message crossing on the identical traffic is 0.040 ms at the median at 128 frames — block P's row, with a p99 of 0.456 ms — and 0.030 ms with a p99 of 0.32 to 1.88 ms at 1024. That is one to two orders of magnitude inside a burst, and it agrees with block H and with block K to the digit.

**Roughly three fifths of that crossing is the prototype's read pattern, not the batching device — and the probe written correctly would be slower still.** `WsBatchRead._recv_now` creates a fresh task per frame with `asyncio.ensure_future(self.ws.recv())` and gives the link loop up to three `asyncio.sleep(0)` passes before it decides the socket is empty, while every shape it is compared against — the pending flag, per message, the `beat` drain and the off-loop shape — reads with a plain `async for`. Measured for this revision over a local `websockets` link at a load of 0.31 to 0.44, eight bursts of 1024 frames, sender and reader in one process so that only the ratio transfers: draining a burst takes a median of **4.52 ms** under `async for`, **11.27 ms** under `_recv_now`'s `asyncio.sleep(0)` probe, and **24.86 ms** under the `asyncio.wait({task}, timeout=0)` probe #2033 recommends for correctness — factors of 2.5 and 5.5 against the plain read. The batched crossing is essentially the wait for the rest of the batch and so scales with that span. Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions.

- The 9.2 to 9.4 ms quoted above is not the price of batching as such. A reader that could drain the socket at the plain rate would leave about 3.7 ms of it, and the other three fifths belongs to the instrument. #2033's probe comparison set the two probes against each other on batch counts and did not measure read span, so this is not covered there.
- But no such reader exists in a batching adapter, because a batching adapter must probe. Of the two probes that work, the bench used the faster; the one #2033 names as correct would put the crossing nearer 20 ms at a 1024-frame burst. So an implementation written the recommended way costs more than the bench measured, not less.

Either way the direction of the comparison is unaffected and the magnitude should be quoted as a range rather than as a single figure.

**Who waits on that crossing is not answered here.** A 9 ms crossing inside a bulk burst harms nobody unless something latency-sensitive is queued behind it, and this block queues nothing behind it: the sender emits one traffic shape at a time and the harness pushes one frame per beat regardless. The measurement that decides the question is #2033's block K, which injected a second panel's tap half way through each burst and found it held for a median of 1.263 ms at 128 frames and 9.814 ms at 1024 against 0.030 and 0.027 ms per message. This block cannot reach that conclusion from the clock alone and does not claim to.

**So these rows leave the decision where #2033 and #2021 put it.** #2021, #2030 and #2033 all now read cross per message with no batching device, with the capped read named as the thing to add if #2008 asks for it on the headless server. That conclusion rests on #2033's reading of #1916 — a `set` on a grid row carries a row array and a `set` on the grid leaf a whole `cells` object, so a row clear, a fill, a paste and a preset load are each one inbound frame, leaving only #1920's reconnect re-send as an inbound burst — and on block K's held tap. These rows touch neither. They are consistent with that rule, they add no reason to reopen it, and they close the measurement gap #2025 left rather than the argument.

**Both engine-side levers remove the flagged shape's late pulses at 50 Hz, and the control is now contemporaneous.** Block P's unlevered flagged 50 Hz row, in this point's own script copy at a 1.20 gate and a load of 0.57 to 0.52 between two clean baselines, leaves 68 pulses over 1 ms at p99 1.3065 ms and a 1.5730 ms worst pulse. Under a 2 ms spin margin the same shape leaves none at p99 0.340 ms, and under a `SelectSelector` loop none at p99 0.003 ms. The lever rows themselves are block L's and ran at a 1.50 gate at loads of 1.16 to 0.75, so the control is quieter than the rows it controls, which is the safe direction. Block P's 68 late pulses sit above #2025's block F figure of 51 for the same shape and beside its 69 for the unflagged shape, which is the block-to-block spread #2025 and #2033 both report for this row and is another reason not to read one late pulse as a difference anywhere in this finding. What is precise in #2025's table and was overstated in the first draft of this finding: #2025 already carries the flagged shape under both levers at 25 Hz, twice each, so the asymmetry these rows remove is at 50 Hz only.

**The two levers are not equivalent, and the table should not be read as though they were.** Their p99s differ by two orders of magnitude — 0.3403 ms at a 2 ms spin margin against 0.0030 ms under `SelectSelector` — and only the selector row is on the baseline; the spin-margin row's p99 is higher than every burst row in this finding. Both also keep a crossing tail: the worst single crossing was 3.05 ms under the spin margin and 7.89 ms under the selector, on rows whose crossing p99 is a quarter of a millisecond. Neither lever is any cheaper than #2025 and #2021 already price it: the spin margin has no public setter (`subsequence/sequencer.py:465`; the only public control is `disable_spin_wait` at `602`, which turns spin off rather than widening it), and the selector cannot be confined to one loop by a process-wide policy.

**The off-loop shape is on the baseline at a 1024-frame burst, which nothing had measured.** No wake-up, p99 0.0018 ms, a worst pulse of 0.0062 ms, and a "crossing" of 0.0001 ms that is the stopwatch starting and stopping on one thread. It is the cleanest adapter row anywhere in this finding on the worst-pulse column. It costs what #2021 and #2033 say it costs elsewhere — #1925's single-writer invariant, whose version counter, ordered outbound log and last-writer-wins all rest on every write being applied on the loop — and nothing on the clock.

**One figure in these rows must not be read as the drain rule's cost, and the reason is not the one the first draft gave.** The `beat`-drained rows at 1024 frames show a crossing median of 93.5 and 94.8 ms, far below the 243 to 251 ms #2025 measured. The first draft explained that as a burst period of 4 seconds being exactly eight beats at 120 BPM, so that every burst lands at the same phase. The rows refute that explanation: if the phase were constant every crossing would cluster within a few milliseconds, and instead the p95 is 491.9 and 495.1 ms against those medians, a spread of four hundred milliseconds. The sender waits `asyncio.sleep(burst_period)` and only then broadcasts the burst frame by frame, so its period is 4 seconds plus the broadcast and the phase drifts. The real artefact is stronger than the one first offered: **the crossing distribution has an effective sample size of eight, not 8192**, because a whole burst is queued within about twenty milliseconds and then applied at one `beat`, so all 1024 of its crossings carry one arrival's phase. The median of 8192 numbers drawn from eight clusters is the middle cluster, and the p95 and p99 are the largest. That caution applies to every column of those two rows, not to the median alone. The figure that does carry is the bound: the maximum of 499.3024 and 499.2357 ms is under one beat of 500.0 ms and within a millisecond of it, agreement to about a part in seven hundred — not, as the first draft said, "to three digits", since 499 and 500 differ in the third. The drain rule's median is #2025's steady-rate figure of 243 to 251 ms.

## Not covered

- **The socket-work saving read-batching is half recommended for.** No row here counts socket reads, frame assemblies or JSON parses on the link thread, so #2025's second ground is neither confirmed nor refuted by anything measured.
- **A second panel's tap arriving inside a burst.** Not run here; it is #2033's block K, and it is the measurement that decides against the uncapped read.
- **The capped read.** Not run here at any burst size; #2033's block K carries it.
- **Read-batching and the off-loop shape under either engine-side lever.** Block F never carried those rows and neither do these. There is no reason to expect a surprise, since a lever acts on the wake-up and a batching device changes only how many there are, but it is not measured.
- **The paint rate.** No row here is at 25 Hz; the rows block F did not reach are all bursts and levers. The paint rate with every shape and clean baselines is #2033's block J.
- **The return leg of the crossing.** Only link thread to loop is timed. The harness pushes one frame per beat whatever the inbound traffic, so there is no per-tap outbound path to time.
- **The read-span probe against the bench's own conditions.** Its sender and reader share one process, so its absolute spans are not the bench's and only the ratio between the three read patterns is used. What it does not settle is how much of the batched crossing would remain on a two-process link, which needs the bench itself instrumented.
- **The discarded read-batching row at 1024 frames in block L**, whose load drifted from 1.22 to 3.70, and #2025's block F per-message 128-frame row, whose load drifted from 1.01 to 3.77 and which logged 126 late pulses at p99 2.6185 ms against a block-F baseline that itself logged 25. Neither is read for anything; block N replaces the first and block P the second.
- **Tempi other than 120 BPM**, and a real `StepGrid.apply()` in place of the dict write. #2008 and #2009 carry the latter.
