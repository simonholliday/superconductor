  design · active · #playhead · #research · superintendent

Summary: The step highlight should be driven by client-side extrapolation between per-beat anchors rather than by the arrival of server events: the adapter stamps each `beat` with its pulse, its wall-clock time and the current tempo (the listener runs 0.06 to 0.37 ms after that pulse's note_on left the sequencer, measured), the service hands the stamp on in its own clock, the browser converts it with an NTP-style offset from the transport's ping/pong (estimator noise 0.04 ms Chromium, 0.3 ms Firefox on loopback), and a requestAnimationFrame loop computes each pattern's current step for the moment the frame will be shown, from `cycle_start_pulse` and `length_pulses`, which `pattern_reschedule` delivers one lookahead before the cycle. Network jitter then drops out of the playhead position. The error budget is an estimate assembled from measured and cited terms, not an end-to-end measurement: about 5 to 24 ms behind the MIDI send on a wired LAN when the position is computed for the presentation time, of which up to 16.7 ms is the 60 Hz frame and 5 ms the panel's pixel response; computed naively at callback time it is 22 to 40 ms, at the edge of the 45 ms sound-leading detectability threshold of ITU-R BT.1359-1, and a Wi-Fi tail of 50 to 80 ms would breach it if arrival time were used. The tempo slope must come from `current_bpm` or from the interval between anchors, not from `seconds_per_pulse`, which external MIDI clock never updates. The sounding flash rides the realised view of the coming cycle and needs a fallback to the intent grid when that frame is late. A per-pulse engine event was measured to cost the clock nothing and buys nothing over the anchor. Nothing in the engine needs to change for v1; three small read-only exposures (the pulse in the `beat` payload, `cycle_start_pulse` in `live_info()`, a `max_device_latency_ms` property) would remove reads of private names and close one timing ambiguity. Real vsync, the presentation pipeline depth, the Pi and the panel's own input lag are hardware-gated.

**Question.** How does a cell show that it is being played, within the visual timing the eye demands at 60 to 180 BPM, given that the finest server event is `beat` and it fires after that pulse's MIDI has gone out?

## Two things a "playing" cell means

The first use-case needs two layers of feedback, and they have different sources. The **playhead** is the column under the clock: it moves once per step whether or not anything sounds there. The **sounding flash** is the cell that actually plays at that step, which differs from the intent grid when probability, energy gating or a mute act between them (#1914 separates the intent view from the realised view for this reason). Both layers are derived below from one client-side clock; neither needs a per-note event from the sequencer. They differ in one respect that matters on a poor network: the playhead depends only on anchors that describe the past, while the flash depends on a frame that must arrive before the cycle it describes.

## What the sequencer gives today

- **Events and their timing.** `_check_bar_change` and `_check_beat_change` spawn the `bar` and `beat` emissions as tasks before the pulse's MIDI is processed (`sequencer.py:1402-1427`); the tasks run only after `_advance_pulse` returns, which is after `_maybe_reschedule_patterns` and `_process_pulse` have sent the pulse's MIDI and `pulse_count` has been incremented (`sequencer.py:1484-1486`, ordering documented at `1506-1520`). The `beat` payload is the beat within the bar only (`sequencer.py:1427`); the pulse must be inferred from `sequencer.pulse_count`, which the listener sees already incremented. In the unloaded case the beat's pulse is `pulse_count - 1`, but the internal-clock loop's inner `while current_time >= next_pulse_time` (`sequencer.py:1506-1533`) processes every overdue pulse in one pass without yielding unless an await actually suspends, so after a stall the beat task can run with `pulse_count` advanced by two or more. The robust inference is the last multiple of `pulses_per_beat` at or below `pulse_count - 1`, which is what the prototype below used; the exposure proposed at the end removes the inference altogether. `display.py:476-480` records the same ordering: the display always trails the audio by one pulse-processing cycle.
- **Cycle anchors.** `pattern_reschedule(pattern, cycle_start_pulse)` is spawned right after each rebuild (`sequencer.py:1829`), one `reschedule_lookahead` before the cycle starts; the pattern also carries `_cycle_start_pulse` (`pattern.py:145`, written at `sequencer.py:1133`, `1795` and `composition.py:5985`) and `length` in beats (`pattern.py:104-115`). `live_info()` returns each pattern's `length` and `cycle` but not its cycle start pulse (`composition.py:3894-3903`). A pattern registered while running starts at the current pulse (`composition.py:1563`), so a cycle start is not in general a multiple of the length.
- **Tempo.** `set_bpm` recomputes `seconds_per_pulse` (`sequencer.py:644-646`); a `set_target_bpm` ramp recomputes it on every pulse (`sequencer.py:1436-1450`); under Ableton Link the loop recomputes it when the session tempo moves (`sequencer.py:1627-1669`). Under external MIDI clock the pulses arrive from the cable (`sequencer.py:1574-1609`) and `_estimate_bpm` writes only `current_bpm`, rounded to an integer over the last 24 ticks (`sequencer.py:858-882`); `seconds_per_pulse` is assigned nowhere else in the file (`sequencer.py:458`, `646`, `1450`, `1669` are the only writes), so in that mode it holds the constructor tempo for the whole session. A pulse's wall time is therefore predictable from `start_time + pulse * seconds_per_pulse` only in internal-clock mode at constant tempo, and `seconds_per_pulse` is not a safe tempo slope in external-clock mode. A design that anchors on measured pulse times and takes its slope from `current_bpm` or from the anchors themselves works in all four modes.
- **Device latency compensation.** Every event for a device is physically sent `max_latency - device_latency` ms after its logical time, so all devices sound together at logical time plus `_max_device_latency_ms` (`sequencer.py:1948-1990`, README-AGENTS line 131). The highlight should be offset by that maximum, which is private today (`sequencer.py:419`).
- **The clock's own accuracy.** Wake jitter is microseconds with spin-wait (`benchmarks/clock_jitter.py`; measured again below), so the sequencer contributes nothing visible.
- **The legacy dashboard.** `web_ui.py` snapshots `sequencer.pulse_count` into `playhead_pulse` every 100 ms (`web_ui.py:169-196`, `225`) and the page positions a bar at `playhead_pulse % length_pulses` on each frame it receives (`assets/web/index.html:242-243`, `268`); the position therefore jumps in 100 ms steps, which is 0.8 of a 16th-note step at 120 BPM and 1.2 steps at 180 BPM, and the pattern grid is refreshed only on a bar change so that it appears when the cycle starts rather than when it is rebuilt (`web_ui.py:244-247`). That is the behaviour to improve on.

What this settles. The `beat` listener is the right place to take a timestamp: it runs 0.06 to 0.37 ms after the pulse's note_on left `_send_midi`, at every tempo and with or without spin-wait, so `time.time()` taken there is the MIDI send time to within half a millisecond. It also runs well inside the same pulse interval, so in these unloaded runs `pulse_count - 1` was the beat's pulse; the measurement does not test the bare inference, because the prototype floored to the beat boundary and the flooring cannot fail, and it says nothing about the catch-up case described above. `pattern_reschedule` precedes its cycle by the lookahead less about a millisecond, so with the one-pulse lookahead #1915 recommends the client learns a cycle's realised notes 12 to 20 ms before the cycle starts at 120 to 180 BPM, and 40 ms at 60 BPM. The emulated per-pulse event (a `_spawn(emit_async("pulse"))` wrapped around `_advance_pulse`, with a listener that appends a tuple) changed the wake jitter by nothing measurable (median 1 us, p99 2 us in both runs) and its listener ran 0.09 ms median, 1.11 ms max after the pulse: the engine side of a pulse event is affordable; what it would cost to serialise and broadcast 24 to 72 frames a second was not measured here (#1919 measured a 20 Hz fan-out to four clients at nothing measurable on a daemon thread and about 1 ms p99 on the clock loop). At 180 BPM the spin-wait wake jitter was 0.5 ms median in all three runs rather than the microsecond figure seen at 60 and 120 BPM; this was not investigated (it is `serving`'s and `latency`'s territory) and it does not change the panel figure, because the listener-after-send column is what the highlight inherits.

## Measured: the browser side

Method. `scratchpad/playhead/pw_bench.py` starts `pw_server.py` (aiohttp 3.14.3 on 127.0.0.1:8903, outside the reserved ports) serving `bench.html`, and drives headless Chromium 151.0.7922.34 and Firefox 153.0 through Playwright 1.62.0 from the `transport-ui` venv at a 1920 by 1080 viewport. Headless means no display, no vsync and a software compositor, so these figures are indicative of relative cost only; absolute frame pacing and the callback-to-glass pipeline on the panel host are hardware-gated. Results in `results/pw_chromium.json` and `results/pw_firefox.json`.

Rendering. Each case runs about 240 frames with a highlight column either advancing every frame (worst case) or every 125 ms (a 16th at 120 BPM). "Render cost" is the time from the end of the rAF callback to the next macrotask via `MessageChannel`, which approximates style, layout, paint and commit but not presentation. Chromium's rAF interval was 16.7 ms median, 16.8 max in all cases except one 49.9 ms frame in the 1024-cell DOM case; Firefox's was 17.06 median with two frames of 33 ms.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| DOM: toggle a class on the column's cells | 0.4-0.8 / 1.4 ms | 0.3 ms median at 125 ms steps (p95 4.2, one 46.8 ms outlier); 4.2 / 5.4 ms when moving every frame | 0 / 0-1 ms |

All three stay inside a 16.7 ms frame at 1024 cells on this machine; the DOM class toggle only touches the DOM when the step index changes (8 times a second at 120 BPM), the overlay is the cheapest per frame, and a full canvas redraw is the most expensive with no advantage at this cell count. This is the input `ui-rendering` asked for on the playhead specifically; that point measures the grid itself.

Clock offset. 320 `ping`/`pong` exchanges at 20 ms spacing over a loopback WebSocket, offset per sample computed NTP-style as `server_at - (at + rtt / 2)` with `at = performance.timeOrigin + performance.now()` and `server_at = time.time() * 1000`, so the true offset is about zero and the spread measures the estimator. Chromium: round trip 1.2 ms median (two JSON parses and the aiohttp server included), raw offset standard deviation 0.039 ms, spread 0.27 ms; keeping the lowest-round-trip sample per window of eight gave a standard deviation of 0.036 ms; the windowed median 0.02 ms. Firefox: round trip 1 ms median, raw standard deviation 0.32 ms, spread 1.5 ms, min-round-trip 0.26 ms, windowed median 0.19 ms; `Date.now()` and `timeOrigin + now()` disagreed by 1 ms. The Firefox noise is its timer coarsening: Firefox settled on "1ms with jitter" for `performance.now()` after the 2018 timing-attack mitigations (https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1440863, comment 6, and the linked bug "Set Timer Resolution to 1ms with Jitter"), whereas the High Resolution Time specification's figures that MDN quotes, 100 us in non-isolated and 5 us in cross-origin-isolated contexts (https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Performance/now; Chrome 91 onward, https://developer.chrome.com/blog/cross-origin-isolated-hr-timers), are what Chromium implements; #1920 measured the same 0.1 ms and 1 ms steps on this machine. On a real LAN the estimator's error is bounded by half the path asymmetry of the sample used, about 1 ms on a wired segment (#1920 measured 0.5 to 1.8 ms ICMP on this LAN). On Wi-Fi the uplink and downlink queues are not symmetric, so the bound is larger and was not measured; the min-round-trip filter is what keeps a congested sample out, and how well it holds on Wi-Fi is hardware-gated below.

Two consequences. First, the case that matters is the highlight arriving after the drum hit, which is "sound leading" and has the tight 45 ms bound; the realised drum sound also lags the MIDI send by the drum machine's own input latency, which is rig-specific and not measured here and pushes the picture toward "sound delayed", the lenient side. The ITU figures are for television viewers; a musician watching a grid he is tapping is the strict end (the 2022 paper), so the budget should aim well inside 45 ms and treat the 60 Hz frame as the floor it cannot beat. Second, because vision alone is the looser rhythmic sense and the eye reads frame-time irregularity in continuous motion as stutter, the playhead should **step** once per grid step rather than slide continuously: a stepped highlight is judged against the drum hit (the audiovisual bound above), whereas a sliding bar at 60 Hz shows the frame quantisation itself. A sourced detectability threshold for irregularity in a purely visual step sequence was not found; see Not covered.

The legacy 100 ms polling of `web_ui.py` was excluded from the list: its position error alone is 0.4 to 1.2 steps at 60 to 180 BPM.

**Recommendation: B.** It is A with three numbers added to a frame that exists anyway, it removes the network from the playhead position entirely (which is the only way a Wi-Fi panel keeps the column inside 45 ms), it works under tempo ramps, Link and external clock because the anchor is a measured time, and it survives a hidden tab or a reconnect without special handling because the position is a function of absolute time. C was measured to be affordable on the engine side but buys nothing over B and adds an engine event and 24 times the traffic. The flash layer is the same under all three and is handled by a fallback rule, not by the choice.

## The recommended mechanism

**Adapter (in the Subsequence process).** In a `beat` listener: `pulse = ((sequencer.pulse_count - 1) // sequencer.pulses_per_beat) * sequencer.pulses_per_beat`, `at = time.time()`, `bpm = sequencer.current_bpm`; publish `{"t":"event","app":"subsequence","path":"/subsequence","name":"beat","data":{"pulse":pulse,"bar":bar,"beat":beat,"bpm":bpm,"latency_ms":max_device_latency},"at":at_ms}` in #1920's envelope. Do not publish `seconds_per_pulse`: it is stale under external clock. In the `pattern_reschedule` listener: publish per grid `cycle_start_pulse` and `length_pulses` (with the realised view #1914 already sends there). On `stop`: publish it so the client clears the highlight.

**Service.** The browser estimates its offset against the service's clock (`server_at` on `pong`, #1920), so `at` on every event must be in that same clock. In #1919's recommended shape C the adapter and the service share the host's clock and `at` passes through unchanged. In shape B (service on another host) the service estimates its offset to each app over the adapter link with the same ping/pong and min-round-trip filter, and rewrites `at` into service time before fan-out; that adds an app-to-service term (about 0.1 to 1 ms on a wired LAN, unmeasured). Re-stamping on receipt would work too but adds the one-way link delay to every anchor.

**Client.** Offset: from every `pong`, `offset_i = server_at - (at + rtt/2)`; keep the sample with the smallest `rtt` from the last eight (the 2 s ping cadence of #1920 gives a fresh sample every 16 s; during the first seconds after connect send pings at 100 ms until eight are in hand), and discard any sample whose `rtt` exceeds the best seen this session by more than a couple of milliseconds, holding the previous estimate instead. Slope: `pulse_ms = (anchor.at - previous.at) / (anchor.pulse - previous.pulse)` once two anchors are in hand and the result is within ten percent of `60000 / (bpm * 24)`; otherwise, and whenever a `bpm` `changed` frame arrives between beats (the OSC `/bpm` path can change tempo without a ramp), use `60000 / (bpm * 24)` until the next anchor pair. Under external clock `bpm` is an integer estimate, so the anchor-interval slope is the accurate one there. Position: on each rAF frame with timestamp `ts`, compute for the moment the frame will be shown, `t_present = performance.timeOrigin + ts + frame_ms`, where `frame_ms` is the median rAF interval measured by the page itself (16.7 at 60 Hz); then `t_server = t_present + offset - latency_ms - visual_offset_ms`, `pulse_now = anchor.pulse + (t_server - anchor.at) / pulse_ms`; for each grid on the page `step = floor(((pulse_now - cycle_start_pulse) mod length_pulses) / (length_pulses / cols))`; move the column highlight only when `step` changes. If the host's pipeline turns out to be two frames deep, computing for one frame ahead leaves the highlight one frame late (the strict side); computing for two on a one-frame host puts it one frame early (the lenient side), so when in doubt lean early. Flash: light the sounding cell from the realised view for the cycle whose `cycle_start_pulse` contains `pulse_now`; until that cycle's realised view has arrived, light it from the intent grid, and switch when the view lands (it precedes the cycle by 12 to 40 ms on the LAN and can miss the first steps on a Wi-Fi tail). Staleness: if no `beat` has arrived for two beats' worth of `pulse_ms`, dim the playhead rather than let it run on.

**Why the highlight is aligned to the MIDI send, with an offset.** The sequencer's authoritative moment is the send; the audible hit follows by `max_device_latency_ms` (compensated, so uniform across devices) plus the instrument's own MIDI-to-sound time, which is rig-specific and belongs in the composition file or the panel's settings under #1465, never in the engine. The client subtracts the first from the anchor and exposes the second as a user-adjustable visual offset.

As a fraction of a 16th-note step the compensated worst case of B is 9 percent at 60 BPM, 19 percent at 120 and 28 percent at 180; uncompensated it is 16, 32 and 48 percent. A change of tempo not yet anchored costs at most one beat of drift: 1 BPM at 60 BPM is 16.4 ms, at 120 BPM 4.1 ms, at 180 BPM 1.8 ms. The 60 Hz frame is two thirds of the compensated worst case and cannot be reduced in software; a 120 Hz host would halve it. The budget is a workstation estimate: the real frame cadence, the pipeline depth, the panel's refresh rate and input lag, and the browser-to-service path on the panel host are hardware-gated below.

## What the sequencer should expose

Nothing is required for v1: the adapter reads `pulse_count`, `pulses_per_beat` and `current_bpm` (public), each grid pattern's `length` (public) and `_cycle_start_pulse` (private, `pattern.py:145`), and `_max_device_latency_ms` (private, `sequencer.py:419`). Three small read-only additions would remove the private reads and are in the same spirit as #1914's `live_info()` extensions: the pulse in the `beat` payload alongside the beat-in-bar (`sequencer.py:1427`), which also removes the `pulse_count` inference and its catch-up ambiguity; `cycle_start_pulse` in each `live_info()` pattern entry (`composition.py:3894-3903`); and a `max_device_latency_ms` property. A per-step or per-pulse engine event is not needed and, if ever wanted, was measured to cost the clock nothing on the engine side.

## Not covered

- Frame pacing under real vsync and the callback-to-glass pipeline depth, on the panel host and on a Raspberry Pi: headless Chromium and Firefox on the workstation ran at 16.7 and 17.06 ms cadences with a software compositor, which says nothing about a Pi's GPU path.
- The drum machine's MIDI-in-to-sound latency, which shifts the audible hit later than the MIDI send; it is rig-specific and outside this research.
- A sourced detectability threshold for irregularity in a purely visual step sequence (visual anisochrony): Grondin and McAuley 2009 (Perception 38) and the Grondin 2010 review were behind paywalls or cookie walls to this session, so only the auditory-dominance statement and the continuous-motion frame-timing study are cited.
- Absolute audiovisual temporal-order thresholds for flash-and-click stimuli: the primary papers found (Zampini, Shore and Spence 2003; Vatakis and Spence 2008; Vroomen and Keetels 2010) were behind paywalls or returned 403, so only the ITU, Friberg and Sundberg and O'Donohue figures are cited.
- The panel's refresh rate: the datasheet states horizontal sync 30 to 83 kHz and no vertical figure; 60 Hz is assumed.
- The 0.5 ms median wake jitter seen at 180 BPM with spin-wait on this workstation was noted, not explained.
- The app-to-service offset over the adapter link in shape B: no second host was available to run Python on.

## Hardware-gated

- rAF cadence, dropped frames, callback-to-glass depth (one or two vsyncs) and DOM versus overlay render cost in the panel host's actual browser on Windows, a Linux laptop and Raspberry Pi OS, at 1920 by 1080 fullscreen; the depth sets the `frame_ms` compensation constant.
- The refresh rate the host drives the panel at, and the panel's input lag (signal to pixel), which needs a photodiode or high-speed camera against the drum machine's audio output; the 5 ms figure is pixel response only.
- Clock-offset estimate stability from the panel host over the real wired and Wi-Fi paths, and whether the min-round-trip filter holds the estimate within 1 ms on Wi-Fi.
- In shape B, the app-to-service offset over the adapter link.
- A perceptual check by Simon of highlight-versus-hit alignment at 120 and 180 BPM with the visual offset at zero and at the drum machine's measured latency.
