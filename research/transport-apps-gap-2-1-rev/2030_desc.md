Corrected 2026-09-04 by #2033: the recommendation has moved from C to B, because the burst the batching device collapses is not one the envelope in force produces.

Why it matters. #1928 fixes Subsequence's inbound rule as a bounded queue "drained in the `reschedule_pulse` and `beat` listeners" — that is #2021's column A, and it puts the `ack`, and therefore the cell's face, a measured 243 to 251 ms behind the finger at 120 BPM with a maximum of 497 to 500 ms. #2021 recommends crossing promptly instead, and #2018's version of that column carries a pending flag: schedule the drain only when one is not already pending. #2025 measured the flag and it never fires at any rate a hand or a browser produces — 1.000 messages per wake-up and a largest batch of one at 5, 25, 50 and 200 Hz — and on a burst it catches about eight or nine frames rather than the burst. So the rule #1928 documents is not the rule #2021 now recommends, and neither is the flag; this is the sentence in #1928 that has to change, and it is Simon's to settle because it is the app-side half of the crossing decision he is already being asked to make.

Answer this after #2021, because it only arises if that answer is B. Under A, #1928's rule stands unchanged.

What changed on 2026-09-04. This spike used to recommend C, batching at the socket read. #2033 withdrew that after re-reading #1916: the contract in force already defines a `set` on a grid **row** (a row array replacing the row) and on a grid **leaf** (a whole `cells` object), so a row clear, a row fill, a paste and a preset load are each **one** inbound frame, not one per cell — and a page-load seed travels outbound as `snapshot`, while an algorithm's bulk write never leaves the process. The only inbound burst the envelope actually produces is #1920's reconnect re-send of pending sets, bounded by its 5 s pending timeout at roughly a hundred frames. Crossing that burst per message cost one late pulse of about 1.2 ms in 1536; batching it cost none. One late pulse is the whole measured gain of the device, and it is bought with a longer lone crossing, a crossing tail inside the burst, another panel's tap held behind the batch, an unbounded batch in front of a bounded queue, and a probe that fails silently.

The implementation trap, measured, and it stands whichever way this goes. A read-batching adapter must probe for a further frame without cancelling the read it is probing. `asyncio.wait_for(ws.recv(), 0)` looks like the natural probe and never batches anything: CPython wraps the coroutine in a task, finds it not done because it has not started, and cancels it (`/usr/lib/python3.12/asyncio/tasks.py:472-520`, the branch at 507), so the probe always times out. Measured over a real `websockets` link at bursts of 16, 128 and 1024 frames, that shape gave a largest batch of one every time, while one long-lived `recv` task probed with `asyncio.wait({task}, timeout=0)`, which never cancels (`/usr/lib/python3.12/asyncio/tasks.py:522-563`), took the whole burst in a single crossing at all three sizes. `websockets` documents that cancelling `recv` is safe and loses no data, so the trap is not lost frames but a device that quietly does nothing.

Two rules belong with C if it is ever taken. **The read batch must be capped**, because the queue behind it can only drain once the batch ends: an uncapped batch in front of #1928's bounded queue truncates a single bulk edit in the middle rather than shedding load at the edge. **The apply drain must be bounded by whole frames, never inside one**, and continue with `call_soon`, which appends to the loop's ready queue without writing the self-pipe byte (`/usr/lib/python3.12/asyncio/base_events.py:785`, `814-818`, against `838-846`) and therefore costs no wake-up — because a `reschedule_pulse` listener can run between two continuations and would otherwise consume a half-applied row. Under B neither rule is needed: every frame is its own callback and is applied whole.
