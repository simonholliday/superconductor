# A step tap's visual acknowledgement costs one to two browser frames plus a vsync and the panel's own touch-down latency, about 22 to 56 ms before that unmeasured term against a 69 ms tapping threshold; a pad hit costs about 2 to 45 ms from browser event to sound on one host, where the audio buffer and, in Chromium, frame-aligned touch dispatch are the largest terms, so the 10 ms musical standard is out of reach and 20 to 30 ms on wired Ethernet is the realistic v1 budget; the network is under 2 ms of either

Summary: The literature puts the just-noticeable latency for direct-touch tapping at 64 to 69 ms (Jota 2013, Deber 2015) and for a percussive instrument at 10 ms with 1 ms of jitter as the standard (Wessel and Wright 2002), with 20 ms already rated worse than 10 ms by percussionists (Jack et al. 2016) and 20 to 30 ms as the detection threshold for a continuous instrument (Mäki-Patola 2004). Measured in headless Chromium 151 and Firefox 153 on the workstation, a pointer event reaches its handler in 0 to 2 ms with a mouse or with Firefox's synthetic touch, but in Chromium's synthetic touch path it arrives 0.5 to 18 ms later, tracking the frame boundary, so the class change is committed one frame after that: input to commit is 17 to 34 ms for Chromium touch against 1 to 18 ms for mouse and 0 to 20 ms for Firefox touch. The WebSocket `fire` leaves the page 0.1 ms after the class change, reaches an aiohttp service in 0.45 ms and a second-process adapter on loopback in 1.0 ms median, 1.4 ms p99. Adding the sibling findings (0.24 ms into Subsample's virtual port, under 1 ms dispatch, an output buffer of 2.7 to 23 ms per period with PortAudio running several periods) gives a pad path of about 2 to 45 ms from the browser's event timestamp to the DAC on one host with the buffer at 128 to 512 frames (up to 58 ms with Chromium's two-frame touch dispatch), about 1 ms more across a wired LAN, before the panel's own touch scan, which is unmeasured and bounded only by Microsoft's 35 ms touch-down guideline. A step tap's visual acknowledgement is the browser term plus one vsync and 5 ms of pixel response, 22 to 56 ms before the panel's touch-down latency, inside the 69 ms threshold only if that term stays under about 15 ms or the browser stays at one frame. The transport is not where either budget goes: a grid tap's audible effect is the cycle (#1915), a Substation band switch is about 2 s plus a device reopen or about 16 s with RTL-SDR calibration (#1938), and over a WAN pads are impractical while the grid, whose acknowledgement is local, is unaffected.

**Question.** What are the latency budgets for the two cases the brief names, visual acknowledgement of a step tap and a pad hit heard from the hardware, and what does each hop actually cost?

Three consequences. The two cases have thresholds an order of magnitude apart: a tap's visual echo has about 64 to 69 ms before a typical user notices it, while a pad has 10 ms before a percussionist does and 20 ms before the instrument is rated worse. Jitter is judged more harshly than latency in the audio case, and the browser's frame quantisation is a jitter source of up to one frame. And the audio threshold is one no touchscreen driven through a browser will meet, since the panel's own touch scan and one 60 Hz frame together exceed it; the brief's "as soon as possible" is the right framing and the numbers below say what that is.

## Method

Measured on the development workstation (Intel i7-9700K, Linux 6.8.0-138, Python 3.13.11, load average 0.3 to 0.5) on 2026-09-03 with research prototypes under the scratchpad `latency/` directory, not house style: `page.html` is a pad and a 128-cell grid with a `pointerdown` handler that changes a class, sends a `fire` frame in #1920's envelope over a WebSocket and records `event.timeStamp`, `performance.now()` at the handler, after the class change and after `send()`, then in the next `requestAnimationFrame` and in a `MessageChannel` task after it (the approximate commit, the method #1924 used); `pad_chain.py` runs an aiohttp 3.14.3 service on 127.0.0.1:8912 that stamps each `fire` and forwards it to an adapter written as #1928 prescribes, a `websockets` 17.1 client on its own daemon thread with its own loop, which stamps arrival; Playwright 1.62.0 drives headless Chromium 151.0.7922.34 (with `has_touch`, `page.touchscreen.tap`, and with `page.mouse.click`) and Firefox 153.0 (both kinds) at 1920 by 1080, 200 taps at 60 ms spacing per case, after ten warm-up taps and a 40-sample ping/pong clock-offset estimate whose min-RTT sample is kept (offset 0.04 to 0.09 ms in Chromium, 0.6 to 1.2 ms in Firefox, whose `performance.now()` is coarsened to 1 ms so its cross-clock figures carry about 1 ms of noise and small negative minima). Two full runs (`results/run1_pad_chain_*.json`, `results/pad_chain_*.json`, summaries in `results/pad_chain_run1.txt` and `pad_chain_run2.txt`), a third Chromium touch run with `--disable-features=RafAlignedTouchInputEvents,RafAlignedMouseInputEvents` (`results/pad_chain_chromium_touch_noraf.json`) and a control run; `budget.py` assembles the tables below from these and the cited terms (`results/budget.txt`). `event.timeStamp` is "the time (in milliseconds) at which the event was created" in the same time base as `performance.now()` (https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Event/timeStamp), so input-to-handler measures the browser's own queueing of a synthetic event, not the panel's. No MIDI port and no audio device was opened; the MIDI and audio terms are the sibling findings' and Subsample's own.

Four things this settles. First, the page's own work is nothing: class change and `send()` together cost 0.1 to 0.3 ms, and the `fire` frame is on the wire before the paint. Second, the two loopback hops from page to service to adapter cost 1.0 ms median and under 1.4 ms at p99 on Chromium, the same order #1920 measured for a browser round-trip and #1928 for the adapter link; JSON parse and forward in aiohttp is inside the 0.5 ms service term. Third, the visual acknowledgement is dominated by the wait for the next frame, 0 to 16.7 ms uniformly for mouse input and for Firefox touch, so input-to-commit is one frame at most there. Fourth, Chromium's synthetic touch path adds a frame: the handler ran 0.5 to 18 ms after the event's timestamp in clusters that follow the frame boundary, and always about 16 ms before the next rAF, which means the event was delivered just after a frame's rAF callbacks and its paint waited a whole frame; input-to-commit was 17 to 34 ms, two frames at p95, in all three Chromium touch runs, and disabling `RafAlignedTouchInputEvents` changed nothing (run `_noraf`: 5.1 / 17.9 / 18.2 to the handler, 21.7 / 34.1 / 34.6 to commit). Chrome's own statement is that discrete events "will be dispatched right away" and only continuous ones are aligned to rAF (https://developer.chrome.com/blog/aligning-input-events), so the extra frame is either the compositor-thread touch path or an artefact of injecting touches through the DevTools protocol in a headless renderer; which it is can only be settled with a finger on the panel and is the first hardware-gated item below. If it is real, Chromium costs a pad hit and a cell's echo one frame more than Firefox on the same host, which is 17 ms of both latency and jitter and is material to `ui-host`'s browser choice.

## Case 1: visual acknowledgement of a step tap

The cell lights from the local pending state on `pointerdown` (#1920, #1925, #1927); the network, the service and Subsequence are not on this path, and the `ack` arrives when the set is applied at the rebuild (#1925), which confirms or corrects a mark the user has already seen. Against the thresholds: with a one-frame browser the whole path stays under Jota's 64 ms and Deber's 69 ms as long as the panel's own touch-down latency is under about 25 ms, and inside Jota's 40 ms window only when it is under a few milliseconds, which no touchscreen is. With the two-frame Chromium behaviour the path exceeds 69 ms whenever the panel term exceeds about 13 ms. So the budget the design should hold is: one browser frame from event to commit (act on `pointerdown`, never `pointerup`, never wait for the `ack`, as #1927 and #1920 already say), and the panel's touch-down latency measured before the browser is chosen. There is nothing further to gain in JS: the paint cannot precede the event, and the frame and vsync are the display's. A 120 Hz host would halve two terms, 25 ms in total; the iiyama drives 60 Hz as far as is known (#1924).

Against the thresholds: on one host with a one-frame browser and `buffer_frames` at 128 or 256, the software path lands the note in Subsample's mixer in about 3 ms and at the DAC in about 2 to 26 ms, which is Lester and Boley's "Good" to "Fair" band for drums and the region where Jack et al. found 20 ms rated worse than 10 ms; at 512 frames it is 10 to 43 ms, "Fair" at best; at 1024 it is past every musical threshold cited. The two-frame Chromium behaviour adds a median 16 ms and up to 17 ms of jitter on top of whichever buffer, which alone is sixteen times Wessel and Wright's 1 ms jitter bound. The panel's own touch-down latency then sits on top of everything and is the largest unknown. So the 10 ms standard is not reachable by this product on any host: one 60 Hz frame of input jitter plus the panel's scan exceed it before any audio buffer is added. What is reachable is about 20 to 30 ms from finger to sound on wired Ethernet with a one-frame browser and a 128 or 256 frame buffer, with a jitter of one frame from the browser and one touch-report interval from the panel; that is the band the brief's "as soon as possible" resolves to, and it is the band musicians in the cited studies rated as noticeable but usable. Direct MIDI from a co-located service (#1939's second option) would save the 0.5 ms adapter hop and nothing else; the service's own hop is 0.45 ms; neither is where the time goes.

Jitter deserves its own line. The fixed terms (audio buffer, ALSA hop, dispatch) are stable to well under a millisecond; the variable ones are the browser frame (0 to 16.7 ms, or 0 to 34 ms with the Chromium touch behaviour), the panel's report interval (4 to 10 ms at 100 to 250 Hz, unmeasured), the network (0.1 ms p99 spread on loopback, about 1 ms on a wired LAN, tens of ms on Wi-Fi). Subsequence's own MIDI path is not on the pad path, but for the record its clock jitter is microseconds with spin-wait (#1924 measured 1 to 2 us median, and `sequencer.py:461-465` spins the last 1 ms) and it compensates a device's fixed output latency by deferring faster devices (`sequencer.py:1949-1992`; `composition.py:3021`, whose docstring example is `comp.midi_output("Subsample", name="sampler", latency_ms=20)` at `3058`), so a sampler whose buffer is 20 ms sounds in time with the drum machines when driven by the sequencer; a pad hit from the panel has no such compensation and should not, since there is no earlier moment to send from.

## The sequencer step tap, for completeness

Its audible latency is the cycle by design: with the default one-beat lookahead a tap lands after one to five beats (500 to 2500 ms at 120 BPM) depending on when in the cycle it is applied, and after one pulse to one cycle plus a pulse with the one-pulse lookahead #1915 recommends (21 to 2021 ms at 120 BPM); the rebuild costs about 0.1 ms per 16 by 8 grid. The write path adds nothing measurable: the adapter queues the set and the sequencer drains it at `reschedule_pulse` and `beat` at a measured p99 of 0.002 ms on the clock (#1928, #1926), and the co-located service leaves Subsequence's median and mean jitter unchanged unless it shares a core (#1929). The `ack` therefore arrives up to one cycle after the tap, which is why the cell's face is the local mark and the ring is the pending request (#1925). The playhead's error budget is #1924's: 5 to 24 ms behind the MIDI send on a wired LAN with the 60 Hz frame the largest term.

## Substation band switching

From #1938 and #1939: the recommended switch (stop the running `RadioScanner` on its own sentinel, construct a fresh one in the same process) measured 37 to 41 ms from request to the new band's first processed slice with the package's file device on the workstation, then the ten-slice noise-floor warm-up, 2 s on a live device; a process restart adds about 0.75 s of imports and drops the adapter link; on an RTL-SDR with `calibration_frequency_hz` set, both routes add about 13.7 s of calibration; the in-place retune that would avoid the reopen and calibration is deferred. Against Nielsen's limits, a 2 s switch is past the 1 s flow limit and needs the `select` to show pending at once and `changed` when the first slice lands; a 16 s calibrated switch is past the 10 s attention limit and needs progress, which `noise_floor` with `warmup_complete` supplies at the end and nothing supplies during calibration; disabling calibration in a panel-switched deployment, as #1939 suggests, brings the switch back to about 2 s plus the device reopen. Device reopen cost and the whole switch on real hardware are hardware-gated in #1938.

## What is and is not practical over a WAN

From #1920's measurements: a WebSocket round-trip of 15 ms median to a nearby endpoint and about 100 ms to a distant one, on a 5 to 25 ms last mile, with a home Wi-Fi tail of 50 to 80 ms when the channel fluctuates. The grid is unaffected because its acknowledgement is local and its audible effect is the cycle; the `ack` and `changed` frames arrive 15 to 100 ms later than on the LAN, which the pending ring absorbs. The playhead is unaffected in position because #1924 anchors it to timestamps rather than arrival, though a Wi-Fi tail can make the sounding flash miss a cycle's first steps. A pad hit gains 7 to 50 ms one way plus the Wi-Fi tail, so its total is 30 to 100 ms or more with jitter of tens of milliseconds, past every musical threshold above, and the specification already accepts that pads on such a network are not practical; the unreliable side-channel #1920 deferred would trim TCP's head-of-line stalls under loss but not the path. Substation and Subsample's waveforms, lists and faders are unaffected at these scales.

## Hardware-gated

- The panel's touch report rate and touch-down latency: `evtest` timestamps for the rate (already listed in #1917), and for the latency an acoustic measurement in the manner of Microsoft's touch HLK tool, which records a fingernail tap and the device's response with one microphone (https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-hardware/design/component-guidelines/touch-response-latency--down-): with `page.html` playing a click or with the drum machine's output in the same recording, one audio file gives finger-to-sound end to end, and with a photodiode or a high-speed camera on the lit cell, finger-to-glass.
- Whether real touch on the panel host shows Chromium's two-frame dispatch: load `page.html` from any server on the panel host's browsers (Chromium kiosk on the Pi 5, Edge on Windows, Firefox) and read `window.records`; the harness's own numbers say what one frame and two look like.
- The rAF cadence, the commit-to-glass depth and the refresh rate driven on the panel host, as #1924 lists.
- `PortAudio output latency` as printed on the sampler host, and `scripts/measure_midi_latency.py` there; then the finger-to-sound recording above with the real service, adapter and Subsample.
- Browser-to-service round-trip from the panel host across the real wired and Wi-Fi segments (#1920's one-liner).
- The band switch on the live RTL-SDR, HackRF and AirSpy (#1938).

## Not covered

- The panel's own input latency and report rate: no figure exists in iiyama's datasheet (#1917) and the panel is not attached to this workstation; the Windows guideline bound is used in the tables and marked as such.
- Whether the Chromium touch dispatch delay is real or a DevTools-injection artefact: not resolvable without a physical touch.
- The primary texts of Jack et al. 2016 and Ng et al. 2012 are behind the ACM paywall from this workstation; their figures are taken from Schmid et al. 2024 and Deber et al. 2015, whose full texts were read.
- PortAudio's period count on the sampler host, which sets the multiple between the README's period table and the real output latency; the startup line is the ground truth and it is hardware-gated.
- No end-to-end measurement joins the browser to Subsample's callback here: the adapter in the harness stops at receipt, and the 0.235 ms MIDI hop is #1937's, measured against a probe port rather than `MidiPlayer`.
