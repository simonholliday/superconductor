## Not covered

- **The tap-to-`ack` figure over a real path.** The crossing decision above is argued from a workstation measurement of the clock cost and from the beat interval, both of which are exact, but the end-to-end figure a player would feel — finger to face across the real LAN, with the service and the composition on the headless server — needs a second host and is #2002. Nothing here waits on it: the drain rule's one-beat bound is arithmetic, not measurement.
- **The coalesced wake-up, as this document described it.** Scheduling one drain per burst rather than one per message was inferred here from the finding's own conclusion that the cost is per wake-up of the clock loop and not per message. Measured while filing #2021, that inference is half right: a pending flag that schedules a drain only when none is outstanding saves nothing, because the loop services the drain in microseconds and the flag is clear again before the next message arrives, so the loop wake-up count equalled the message count in every arm. What saves wake-ups is batching at the socket read — the link thread queues every frame the socket has buffered and crosses once — which was then measured over two paired passes: bursts of eight cells five times a second cost 89 loop wake-ups for 712 messages and left no pulse over 1 ms in 768 in either pass, against 712 wake-ups and five late pulses per-message. The per-message cost at a human tap rate left no pulse over 1 ms in 1536 on the workstation anyway. The figures are on #2021, and the real-server version is still #2008.
