**Question.** How are pages arranged, saved and named on the panel, given that the recommended page model (edited on the panel, saved as files on the service, cached in the browser) has no arrangement mechanism, no service route, no concurrency rule and no way to type a name under the mandatory suppression set?

## What the recommended model actually leaves undesigned

#1916 recommends pages "edited on the panel, saved to the service as a file, cached in `localStorage`", with "Drag-to-arrange on glass" as the reason it wins and "Needs a small save endpoint on the service" as its whole cost (`filed-1916.md:244`); #2018 restates that table with the option relabelled and the cost cell reworded — "A. Edited on the panel, saved as files on the service, cached in the browser (recommended)", "Drag-to-arrange on glass | yes", "Cost | a small save endpoint on the service" (`2018.md:200-206`) — and recommends the same option. Nothing beneath either recommendation designs the drag, the endpoint or the naming. Three loose ends follow.

- **The layout library is unowned.** #1931 lists Gridstack 13.2.0 as usable "Only if pages are arranged on the panel itself rather than in a file; a later decision", and says in its own Not-covered section that "Whether pages are arranged on the panel (which would bring Gridstack back in) or declared in a file belongs to the control-contract point." #1916 does not take it. #2018's deferred list then says "page arrangement by a vendored layout library, which returns only if pages are arranged on the panel rather than declared" — which contradicts the option #2018 itself recommends, where pages *are* arranged on the panel. The decision is taken below.
- **The service has no page route.** #1929 designs the unit file, the configuration file, discovery, logging, packaging and a development mode, and its route table is the prototype's `WebSocketRoute("/ws", …)` plus `Mount("/", StaticFiles(...))` (`scratchpad/serving/proto/srv_starlette.py`). It says only that "Saved pages, if pages persist server-side (see `control-contract`), go under `$XDG_STATE_HOME/superintendent` or the unit's `StateDirectory`, never in the config file, so a redeployed config does not lose a page". There is no list, no read, no save, no reorder, and no rule for two panels saving one file.
- **There is no way to name a page.** The suppression set that #1918 establishes ends with "On-screen keyboard on focus | avoid focusable text inputs; where one exists, `inputmode="none"`" (`filed-1918.md:20`), restated by #2018 as "no focusable text inputs" on every host (`2018_part2.md:100`). With no text input and no keyboard on the panel, no page can be created or named.

## The lattice, and what is not on it

#1916's page document places each widget with `"x": 0, "y": 0, "w": 12, "h": 6` and never says what a unit is (`filed-1916.md:236`). Fix it as one lattice cell of 60 px, the working cell size #2018 sets. The panel is 1920 x 1080 at a 0.249 mm pixel pitch (#1917), so the lattice is exactly 32 columns by 18 rows with nothing left over on either axis, and one lattice cell is 14.94 mm on the glass — above every finger-target figure #1927 records (WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.5's enhanced 44 px, Microsoft's 40 px). A page therefore carries a `lattice` block (`{cols, rows, cell}`) and `x`, `y`, `w`, `h` are integers in that page's own lattice cells. Whether the transport and page bar takes one lattice row from the page area, leaving 32 by 17 cells for widgets, or floats over the page so all 32 by 18 are available, fixes the lattice every page document is authored against; it is Simon's (#2039).

**The lattice places widgets; it does not divide their insides.** A widget's interior is its own layout at its own pixel sizes, and it has to be, because #1927's grid arithmetic already works that way: "Sixteen columns beside an 80 px label column give cells 90 to 115 px wide (22 to 29 mm) … thirty-two columns 45 to 57 px (11 to 14 mm)" (`1927.txt:56`), which is where #2018's "at most 32 steps are shown per grid view" comes from. Welding the lattice cell to the grid step cell would break that: a 32-step view plus a label column would want 33 lattice columns and the lattice is 32 wide. It does not need to. A full-width grid widget spans all 32 lattice columns, or 1920 px, and inside it an 80 px label gutter leaves 32 step cells of 57.5 px — inside #1927's own 45-to-57 px band and not equal to the 60 px lattice cell. Sixteen steps on the same span are 115 px, the top of that band. At the enhanced 44 px target the same span would hold 41 steps, so 32 is a legibility ceiling and never a lattice one. Five assertions in `fit_test.js` hold this arithmetic, and eleven more the fit rule and the cell-size arithmetic below.

One consequence to record: #1916's `w: 12, h: 6` example is illustrative, not dimensioned. A sixteen-step by eight-voice drum grid wants about seventeen lattice columns and nine rows at a 60 px lattice, not twelve by six.

**A page document declares its lattice, so `page_contract` goes to `1.1.0`.** The document gains `order` (integer) and `lattice`, and the units of `x`, `y`, `w`, `h` are stated rather than implied. A document still at `1.0.0` is read without either: `lattice` defaults to the panel's own `{32, 18, 60}` and a page with no usable `order` sorts after every page that declares one, by id — a stated rule rather than a magic default invented in the handler, and asserted by the listing tests. The specification's control-contract point asks for "versioning so an old page survives a new app release" (`superintendent-research-spec.md:155`); this is the first exercise of it.

**A surface that is not the panel.** #1912's decided table settles the host as possibly "a Raspberry Pi, a Windows desktop with a second monitor, or a random laptop" (`superintendent-research-spec.md:21`), so a viewport smaller than the declared lattice is an ordinary case, not an edge. The rule is one line and it never re-flows a page, because re-flowing would silently change what a coordinate means:

    scale = min(1, vw / (cols * cell), vh / (rows * cell))

capped at 1 so a larger surface leaves surplus rather than magnifying the page past the size it was authored at, and uniform below 1 so every widget stays where it was put. Measured against the panel lattice: 2560 x 1440 renders at scale 1 with 640 x 360 px of surplus; 1600 x 900 gives a 50 px cell, still above the enhanced target; 1366 x 768 is height-limited at scale 0.711 for a 42.7 px cell, under it; 1024 x 768 is width-limited at 0.533 for a 32 px cell. The panel is the instrument and the others are viewers, so below 44 px the page says so rather than pretending to be playable.

**If #1998 moves the 60 px cell, the lattice moves with it, and no saved page changes meaning.** #2018 flags the figure as unconfirmed in the sentence this draws it from — "whose arithmetic comes from a retailer specification sheet rather than from use and is exactly what #1998 exists to confirm by hand" — so the design has to survive the answer being no. It does, because the cell is declared in the page rather than assumed by the renderer. The panel's own lattice at any cell size is `floor(1920/cell)` by `floor(1080/cell)` with the remainder as margin: 48 px gives 40 x 22, 56 px gives 34 x 19, 60 px gives 32 x 18, 64 px gives 30 x 16, 72 px gives 26 x 15, 80 px gives 24 x 13. Sixty is the only plausible size that tiles both axes exactly, which is a reason to prefer it and not a reason to depend on it. A page authored at one cell renders at that cell under the fit rule above; it is re-authored at the panel's cell the next time somebody arranges it and saves, which is a visible act rather than a silent reinterpretation.

**A2 (variant). Gridstack for its layout model with the drag layer off.** The touch objections above are all against DDTouch, which `dd-draggable.ts:109-113` attaches only inside `if (isTouch)` and only to `dragEls`, so a `staticGrid` instance driven by a bespoke drag calling `grid.update()` would keep the layout model and none of the touch path. It is still declined, and for a reason that survives switching the drag layer off: the displacement is in the engine, not in DDTouch. `_fixCollisions` resolves every overlap by moving something — the dragged node past the collider, or the collider down — and the loop's only exits are "no collision" and "could not move" (`gridstack-engine.ts:133-166`). There is no option that turns a collision into a refusal, and `float: true` (which Supervisor sets, `client/app.js:234`) only removes gravity and the swap branch; the push at `:160` still fires. What is left to borrow is `collide()` as a rectangle-intersection predicate (`:185`), which is four comparisons.

**Excluded rather than short-listed: interact.js 1.10.28** (MIT, `dist/interact.min.js`, 1,398,773 bytes unpacked, one dependency `@interactjs/types`). Its own documentation describes it as "a JavaScript library for drag and drop, resizing and multi-touch gestures for modern browsers" with "powerful options like inertia and modifiers for snapping and restricting", and states that "by default interact.js doesn't move elements for you. Styling an element so that it moves while a drag happens has to be done from your own event listeners." Grid layout, collision resolution and serialising positions are outside that stated scope — the library does not claim them. Choosing it is choosing the bespoke option and paying a dependency for the pointer plumbing, which was never the hard part.

**Recommendation: B, the bespoke snap-drag with tap-to-place on the same engine.** The one library on offer is not neutral here: it carries an explicit single-contact assumption on a ten-point panel, a `releasePointerCapture` that fights the capture discipline #1927 rests on, and a displacement model that moves widgets the hand did not touch — on an instrument, where knowing without looking that the pad bank is bottom-right is the whole point of a fixed layout. Against that it offers responsive machinery a single fixed-resolution panel does not want. The engine that replaces it is 105 non-comment lines and passes seventeen assertions under node covering snap, refusal, bounds, resize, the second contact, `pointercancel`, leaving edit mode mid-drag, and tap-to-place in both modes. C is not a rival to B but a subset of it: `select()` latches a widget and `place()` moves it to the tapped cell through the same `legal()` and the same commit, with no timer and no slop threshold anywhere, which is what makes the arrangement work on a mouse-only or single-contact host as #1912's multi-touch row requires. D is the honest fallback if the browser work is to be deferred: candidates are already a pure function of the manifests under #1916's rule, so a page picker with declaration-order placement is usable on day one, the page routes below are needed either way, and nothing in the file format changes.

**One thing about Gridstack that is not true yet and will be.** The 300 ms delay before a widget follows the finger is on `master` and in no release: `const touchDragDelay = 300` and `const touchDelayMoveThreshold = 10` sit at `master`'s `dd-touch.ts:29-31`, the drag is armed by `setTimeout(..., touchDragDelay)` at `:145`, and a finger that moves more than 10 px summed over x and y before it elapses calls `onEnd` and cancels the pending drag outright (`:132`) — so a quick arrange drag would not start late, it would not start. `master`'s changelog opens with an unreleased "13.2.0-dev (TBD)" section whose only entry is "mobile: pause to drag/reszie vs scroll behavior" citing issue 2781, the same issue the constant's comment cites, and the released tag's file has neither constant. Both files carry the header "touch.ts 13.2.0", which is how a reader gets this wrong. The comment reads in full, in the source:

```
ms to wait, while touching an item, before starting a drag - lets a quick swipe scroll the
page normally instead of always moving the widget (touch only, no mouse ambiguity - see #2781).
Keep this well under the browser's own long-press threshold (~500ms) so we arm - and start
swallowing `contextmenu` - before it tries to pop its context menu / selection callout.
```

The number in that comment is Gridstack's own issue, not a reference here. Under `touch-action: none` there is no scroll for it to disambiguate against, so vendoring 13.2.0 and never updating is a decision that has to be taken and kept; that is a maintenance obligation the bespoke engine does not have, and it is a reason against A rather than a fact about it today.

## The service's page routes on #1929's route table

The route table becomes, in order (Starlette matches routes in declaration order, taking the first full match and remembering a path-match-with-wrong-method for its 405; `starlette/routing.py:684-702` in the installed 1.6.0):

```python
routes = [
	starlette.routing.Route("/api/pages", pages_list, methods=["GET"]),
	starlette.routing.Route("/api/pages/order", pages_reorder, methods=["POST"], max_body_size=262144),
	starlette.routing.Route("/api/pages/{page_id}", page_read, methods=["GET"]),
	starlette.routing.Route("/api/pages/{page_id}", page_put, methods=["PUT"], max_body_size=262144),
	starlette.routing.Route("/api/pages/{page_id}", page_delete, methods=["DELETE"]),
	starlette.routing.WebSocketRoute("/ws", browser_socket),   # #1920
	starlette.routing.WebSocketRoute("/app", app_socket),      # #1928
	starlette.routing.Mount("/", app=starlette.staticfiles.StaticFiles(
		packages=[("superintendent", "static")], html=True)),
]
```

`Mount("/")` matches everything, so it is last. `max_body_size` is a `Route` parameter in 1.6.0 (`starlette/routing.py:207`, wrapping the handler in `RequestBodyLimitMiddleware` at `:230-231`) and answers 413 "Content Too Large" (`starlette/middleware/body_limit.py:23`). The literal `/api/pages/order` sits above the `{page_id}` routes but cannot shadow a page whose id happens to be `order`, because no page route accepts POST and the reorder route accepts nothing else; both are exercised.

**Carriage.** Three ways to move a page between panel and service.

| | HTTP routes on the same app (recommended) | Frames on the existing browser WebSocket | No server-side pages (`localStorage` only) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cost per save | about 4 ms measured for a fetch POST on loopback against 1 ms for a WebSocket frame (#1920); a page save happens on leaving edit mode, not per beat | cheapest, and irrelevant at this rate | none |
| Conditional semantics | `ETag`, `If-Match`, `If-None-Match`, 304, 412, 428 and 413 come from the protocol and from Starlette | every one of them re-invented in the envelope | none |
| Body size guard | one `max_body_size=` on the route | hand-written against #1920's frame reader | none |
| Notifying the other panel | needs frames on the socket anyway | free | not possible |
| Failure mode | fetch has no timeout of its own; #1920 measured a POST to a stalled server hanging until the page's own 30 s abort, so the panel must use an `AbortController` | shares the socket's stale timer | — |
| Survives another browser, a reimage, a second panel | yes | yes | no (#1916, #2018) |

HTTP wins on everything that is not speed, and speed does not matter for an operation that happens when a hand leaves edit mode. Take the routes, and add two frames to #1920's envelope for the push.

**The routes.**

- `GET /api/pages` — `{"pages": [{"id", "label", "order", "etag", "bytes"}, …]}`, ordered by the rule below. `order` is an integer field inside each page document, so no second index file exists to drift out of step with the directory.
- `GET /api/pages/{page_id}` — the document, with a strong `ETag`; `If-None-Match` matching gives 304. This is what a panel uses on load and after a `page_changed`.
- `PUT /api/pages/{page_id}` — one endpoint for create and replace, distinguished by the precondition: `If-None-Match: *` creates and answers 201, or 412 if the page already exists; `If-Match: "<etag>"` replaces and answers 200, 412 if the file has changed, or 404 if there is nothing to replace — never a silent create. **No precondition at all is refused with 428 Precondition Required**, whose whole purpose is this: "The 428 status code indicates that the origin server requires the request to be conditional. Its typical use is to avoid the 'lost update' problem, where a client GETs a resource's state, modifies it, and PUTs it back to the server, when meanwhile a third party has modified the state on the server, leading to a conflict" (RFC 6585 section 3). A body that is not JSON, or whose `id` differs from the path, is 400; a body over 256 KB is 413.
- `DELETE /api/pages/{page_id}` — 204, under the same `If-Match` rule, 428 without one.
- `POST /api/pages/order` — the whole page strip's order in one conditional request, because `order` lives inside each document and moving one page rewrites several. The body is `{"order": [{"id", "etag"}, …]}`; every named page's ETag must still be current or nothing at all is written and the stale ones are named in a 412. Per-page locks are taken in sorted id order so two reorders in flight cannot deadlock. Without this route a reorder would be N unrelated conditional PUTs with no transaction, and a 412 partway would leave duplicate or gapped `order` values that the listing's tie-break would absorb silently.

Candidate pages need no route. #1916's candidate rule is a pure function of the app manifests, and the client already holds every manifest from #1920's `manifest` frame on connect, so the "add a page" list is computed in the browser.

**Listing order, and files that will not load.** Pages that declare an integer `order` come first in that order; everything else follows by name. A page file that will not parse is listed with `"error": "not_json"`, and — this is the case the principle exists for — a file whose *name* the id rule refuses is listed with `"error": "bad_id"` and the filename it actually has, rather than skipped. #1916 sells this page model on "a file Simon can put in git", so hand-made and hand-renamed files are an expected input; `My Page.json`, `Drums.json` or `drums.v2.json` disappearing from the panel with no message anywhere would be the worst failure this design could have. Both are asserted.

**What happens when two panels save the same page file.** Optimistic concurrency with a strong validator. This is deliberately the opposite discipline from the one #1925 chose for grid cells, and #1925 draws the line itself: "Rejecting stale writes is the right rule for a document; for an instrument the later hand is right by definition." A cell is an absolute set where a stale write is harmless and the later hand is meant; a page arrangement is minutes of hand work that a stale write would erase.

- The `ETag` is the first 32 hex characters of the SHA-256 of the exact bytes on disk, quoted. RFC 9110 section 8.8.3: "An entity tag is an opaque validator for differentiating between multiple representations of the same resource, regardless of whether those multiple representations are due to resource state changes over time, content negotiation resulting in multiple representations being valid at the same time, or both." It is strong in the sense of section 8.8.1 — "A 'strong validator' is representation metadata that changes value whenever a change occurs to the representation data that would be observable in the content of a 200 (OK) response to GET" — because it is a hash of exactly those bytes. Identical bytes give an identical ETag, so a panel that re-saves an unchanged page changes nothing for anyone.
- The whole read-compare-write is held under one `asyncio.Lock` per page id. The service is one process on one loop (#1929), so the lock is the entirety of the mutual exclusion; nothing else can interleave.
- The write is to a temporary file in the same directory, `flush`, `fsync`, then `os.replace`, then an `fsync` of the directory. POSIX requires that "a directory entry named *new* shall remain visible to other threads throughout the renaming operation and refer either to the file referred to by *new* or *old* before the operation began", so no reader ever sees a truncated page and a crash mid-save leaves the previous page intact. On failure the temporary file is unlinked; the tests assert nothing but `.json` files are left behind.
- The loser gets **412 Precondition Failed** — RFC 9110 section 15.5.13, "The 412 (Precondition Failed) status code indicates that one or more conditions given in the request header fields evaluated to false when tested on the server" — with the *current* ETag in the body, not merely a refusal. The panel then offers the person the two honest choices: take theirs (load and discard the local arrangement) or keep mine (re-`PUT` with the ETag the 412 named). It must never silently drop an arrangement someone just made with their hands. Which of the two the panel offers is Simon's (#2038).
- After every successful write the service broadcasts on #1920's browser socket. `{"t":"page_changed","id":"drums","op":"saved","etag":"\"…\"","by":"panel","client":"panel-7f3a"}`, with `op` one of `created`, `saved` or `deleted` and a null `etag` on a delete; and `{"t":"pages_reordered","order":[…],"etags":{…},"by":"panel","client":"panel-7f3a"}` after a reorder. `op` is what makes the page bar correct on a second panel: content changes alone would leave a create or a delete made elsewhere invisible for ever, because a page nobody is showing is routed to nobody by #1920's `hello`. `by` is the hand and `client` the client id, which is how #1920 uses those two fields (`hello` carries `"client":"panel-7f3a"` and `changed` carries `"by":"panel"` or `"by":"algorithm"`, `filed-1920.md:133`, `:146-147`).

**Ids and the file layer.** A page id is `[a-z0-9][a-z0-9_-]{0,63}`, checked in the handler and not trusted from the route's `{page_id}` convertor, whose regex is `[^/]+` (`starlette/convertors.py:20-21`) and therefore admits `..`. The file is `<id>.json` in one flat directory, so there is no path to traverse in the first place; the realpath guard Supervisor needed for its `/audio/<relpath>` route (`/mnt/dev/Apps/Supervisor/supervisor/core.py:149-152`) has nothing to guard here. Exercised at exactly the codes they return: `Drums`, a 65-character id, `.hidden` and `drums.json` are refused 400 by the handler, while `..` and `../etc/passwd` never reach it and are 404 from the router.

**Where the files live.** `$STATE_DIRECTORY/pages/` when systemd provides it — `StateDirectory=` creates the directory below `/var/lib/` for a system unit and below `$XDG_STATE_HOME` for a user unit and sets `$STATE_DIRECTORY` to the full path — otherwise `$XDG_STATE_HOME/superintendent/pages/`, which "defines the base directory relative to which user-specific state files should be stored", defaults to `$HOME/.local/state`, and is specified to hold "current state of the application that can be reused on a restart (view, layout, open files, undo history, …)". Otherwise a `pages_dir:` in #1929's configuration file. Under `ProtectSystem=strict` the state directory is writable and the rest of the hierarchy is not. #1948 asks Simon whether page files belong beside the composition instead; only the root changes, none of the above does.

**Who may save.** Nobody is authenticated: any browser that can reach the service can PUT or DELETE any page. That is inside #1912's security decision ("everything is on one private network") and #1922's record of inherited exposure, but it is a new destructive surface rather than an inherited one, and it should be named when a remote mode is designed rather than discovered then.

**Verification.** `pages_routes.py` under the scratchpad implements this against Starlette 1.6.0 with the broadcast injected as a hook, and `pages_test.py` exercises it through `TestClient` with no port bound: 42 assertions pass, covering create, double-create, read, 304, the ordered listing, the missing precondition, the successful save, the save aimed at nothing, the stale save with the current ETag returned, the loser's content not landing, the idempotent re-save, the id rules at their exact codes, the oversize body, the conditional delete, the whole-strip reorder and its all-or-nothing refusal, the reorder route not shadowing a page called `order`, every `page_changed` and `pages_reordered` frame including the null ETag on a delete and the silence after a refusal, the corrupt file and the badly-named file both being listed, and the absence of temporary files.

## The browser's half of the same loop

#1916 makes the cache half of the recommended model, "with the service file authoritative and the browser cache only for the last-open page", which #2018 restates as "the browser cache holding only the last-open page"; the server half above only works if the client half is designed with it. One key holds `{id, etag, doc, dirty}` for that one page; a second holds what the page bar needs to draw, `{pages: [{id, label, order, etag}]}`, and never the documents.

- **Boot draws the cache first and reconciles second**, so nothing on the panel waits for the LAN. Then `GET /api/pages/{id}` with `If-None-Match` on the cached ETag: 304 confirms the cache; 200 with a clean cache replaces it; 200 over unsaved local work is a conflict that draws the local work and holds the server's copy beside it; 404 with a clean cache opens the picker, and 404 over unsaved work turns the next save into a create.
- **With the service unreachable the cached page is drawn and the state says so**, rather than a blank panel. The cached ETag is not a claim about the service, it is a precondition the service will judge, so a save built on a cached copy is still a legal save.
- **Every save the panel can build carries a precondition** — `If-Match` when it holds an ETag, `If-None-Match: *` when it does not — so 428 is unreachable from the panel and exists for anything else that reaches the route.
- **A 412 keeps the work and marks it unsaved**, remembering the ETag the refusal named; "keep mine" is that same document re-sent against that ETag, a deliberate act and never an automatic retry.
- **The page bar is corrected by the frames**: a `page_changed` for a page the bar knows updates its ETag in place, a `deleted` removes it, and a `created` is the one frame that costs a `GET /api/pages`, because the bar knows the new id but not its label or order. A `pages_reordered` reorders in place unless it names a page the bar has never seen.

`cache.js` under the scratchpad is that state machine and `cache_test.js` exercises it: 21 assertions pass over nine reconciliation cases from a cold boot to a page deleted under unsaved work, three on which precondition a save carries, three save outcomes, keep-mine, and five frame cases.

## Creating and naming a page with no keyboard

**Why the panel carries its own naming.** #1921 recommends a Raspberry Pi 5 on Pi OS labwc with Chromium `--kiosk`, and on that host squeekboard cannot appear over the browser: a Raspberry Pi engineer states the mechanism — "if an application requests to be 'fullscreen' then labwc will actually display it full-screen, hiding things like the panel and OSK" — and the labwc issue "[layer-shell] Squeekboard not visible over fullscreen or kiosk Chromium (Wayland, Labwc)" is closed with no fix version visible. But that is a consequence of the kiosk choice, not a property of the host, and the same engineer's recommended remedy is to abandon true fullscreen: "the best solution would be to have the browser run in kiosk mode but as a maximised window rather than actually fullscreen. Then change labwc configuration to hide window decorations/titlebar and disable the panel." The other reported route keeps fullscreen and rebuilds squeekboard: one user compiled the Raspberry Pi fork, set `gsettings set org.gnome.desktop.a11y.applications screen-keyboard-enabled true`, and reports the keyboard then showing and hiding automatically "in kiosk fullscreen mode"; the engineer adds "We have a lot of downstream patches to squeekboard, so we could add this overlay/top configuration option in rpi-os even if upstream squeekboard don't want it."

So an OS keyboard on the panel is available, at a price: give up the fullscreen kiosk that #1921 chose for its lockdown, or carry a forked squeekboard build. Neither price is worth paying for naming a page, and neither helps the other two hosts, which have their own keyboards and their own suppression. Against that, an OS keyboard on the `top` or `overlay` layer is a surface the page cannot control and may cover the field being typed into — the engineer names that trade-off directly: "Making squeekboard an overlay has the downside that it might be on top of the field you want to type in". The panel therefore carries its own means of entering a name, and #1921's `--kiosk` recommendation stands unchanged.

| | A. Generated names only | B. A page-drawn key grid, no input element (recommended) | C. `<input inputmode="none">` plus the same key grid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Creating a page | tap a candidate; id and label come from the candidate | same | same |
| Renaming | not on the panel; edit the file | on the panel | on the panel |
| Inside the suppression set | trivially | yes: the keys are cells, the field is a `<div>`, nothing is focusable and no `inputmode` is needed | no: it is exactly the focusable text input #1918 rules out |
| Works on the recommended host | yes | yes | yes, but `inputmode="none"` buys nothing there, because with `--kiosk` no OS keyboard can appear anyway |
| Works where a keyboard is attached | yes | yes, and typing works too: a hardware keystroke needs no focusable element, so a document-level `keydown` is inside the suppression set, which forbids focusable text inputs and says nothing about keystrokes | yes, and there `inputmode="none"` actively suppresses a touch keyboard that would otherwise be usable |
| Cost | none | 65 non-comment lines of naming, key layout and keystroke mapping, plus the key widget, which is the cell widget with a letter on it | the same, plus an input element and its focus management |
| Reuse | — | the same grid serves any later text need (a filter, a path) | as B |

Two of the three hosts #1912 names have a keyboard attached — "a Windows desktop with a second monitor, or a random laptop", and #1921's B2 variant is exactly the panel as a second monitor on that desktop — so the key grid and the physical keyboard drive one state machine: `keyFromEvent` maps Backspace, Enter, Escape, space and any single `[a-z0-9-]` (upper case carrying its own shift) onto the same `applyKey` a drawn key calls, and ignores anything modified or non-textual. On the panel a name is tapped; on a desktop it is typed; nothing branches beyond that one mapping.

**Excluded rather than short-listed: the VirtualKeyboard API**, which offers `navigator.virtualKeyboard.show()`, `overlaysContent` and the `virtualkeyboardpolicy` attribute, because it has limited availability, is experimental, and requires a secure context — which the v1 plain-HTTP LAN service is not, the same reason #1956 already records for the panel's other HTTPS-only capabilities.

**Recommendation: B, with A as its default so the common case types nothing.** Adding a page opens the candidate list derived by #1916's rule; tapping one creates the page immediately with a generated id and the candidate's own label, and the key grid is opened only to rename. The generated id is `slug(app)-slug(group)`, uniquified with a `-2`, `-3` suffix that respects the 64-character limit; awkward labels (punctuation only, accented, over-long) all yield an id the service's own regex accepts, which the naming tests assert. The id is derived once at creation and never changes, so a rename is a label change and never moves a file, never breaks the browser's last-open cache and never invalidates anything. #2037 asks Simon whether renaming on the panel is wanted in v1 at all; if it is not, this short list collapses to A, no key widget, shift state or label field is written, and nothing else here moves.

The key grid is five rows on the same lattice — digits, two letter rows, a row of shift, the remaining letters, backspace and space, and a row of cancel and done — with wide keys spanning two or three lattice cells. That is 14 by 5 lattice cells, 840 by 300 px at a 60 px cell, and each key is at least 14.94 mm across at the panel's pitch. Eleven assertions cover the id rules, the footprint, shift, backspace, space, the bounded label, cancel and done as states rather than text, and the hardware keystroke path.

## How edit mode is entered, and why no drag can collide with a cell gesture

The collision is real and it is not solvable by arbitration: #1927 sets a cell **the moment the finger lands** ("a `set` `{on: true}` on `pointerdown`"), opens a velocity strip at a 400 ms hold, and — if Simon admits it under #1971 — paints along a row on a slide. Every one of those is a press or a drag on a widget's body. A drag-to-arrange on the same body, arbitrated by time or distance, would have to wait, which #1927 rules out for the tap, or would have to guess, which is worse.

So the modes are made disjoint rather than arbitrated.

- **Entry is a latched control in the page bar, outside the grid.** #2018's visual language already puts "transport and page controls outside the grid in a different shape from cells", so the Arrange control lands on a surface no cell gesture can reach. It is latched and visibly lit, because #1927's own argument against hidden modes applies here too.
- **On entry each widget's body is covered by a scrim.** The scrim is a sibling above the widget content, so a `pointerdown` in edit mode targets the scrim and the cell recogniser is never the event target. A mode flag is the second belt: the arranger's `down()`, `select()` and `place()` all return null unless the mode is `edit`, and the cell recogniser returns early unless it is `play`. Both are asserted.
- **In edit mode there is no hold gesture at all.** A drag starts at the first `pointermove` past 10 px of slop — the same slop #1927 takes from Android's `TOUCH_SLOP`, 2.5 mm at this pitch — with no time threshold. #1927's 400 ms hold therefore has nothing to arm against.
- **In play mode no pointer reaches the arranger.** Tap-on-press and row painting are untouched, in the strong sense that the code that would consume the contact is not attached.
- **Tap-to-place is the same engine without a drag.** Tapping a widget latches it; tapping a lattice cell moves its top-left corner there, refusing an illegal landing exactly as a drop does and keeping the selection so the next tap can try elsewhere. This is how the arrangement works on a mouse or a single-contact host, and it is what the person will reach for anyway when a widget has to cross the screen.
- **Adding and removing widgets is inside edit mode.** A `+` in the page bar opens the bind picker, which is the manifest tree the client already holds; tapping a leaf drops a widget of its default size for that `kind` at the first lattice position the engine's own `legal()` accepts, scanning row-major. A widget's scrim carries a remove affordance. Neither is a gesture; both are taps on chrome.
- **State stays live while arranging.** The playhead keeps running so the arrangement can be judged in motion, and no `set` or `fire` leaves the panel while the mode is latched.
- **Leaving abandons an in-flight drag** and `PUT`s the page; a 1 s debounce after the last commit also saves, so a crash mid-arrangement loses at most the last second. Every such save carries the cached ETag and can be refused: a debounce save answered 412 marks the page unsaved and says so in the page bar rather than interrupting a drag, and the choice between taking theirs and keeping mine is offered when the hand leaves edit mode.

Row reordering in the page strip is the same engine in one dimension, and it writes through `POST /api/pages/order` rather than through a page save, because it changes several documents at once.

## What this changes in the documents it touches

- **#1916.** The page document goes to `page_contract: "1.1.0"` and gains `order` (integer) and `lattice` (`{cols, rows, cell}`); `x`, `y`, `w`, `h` are declared to be lattice cells of the page's own declared cell size; a `1.0.0` document is read with `lattice` defaulted to the panel's and no `order`. The `w: 12, h: 6` example is illustrative and under-sized for a sixteen-by-eight drum grid at a 60 px lattice.
- **#1929.** The route table gains the five `/api/pages` routes ahead of the `Mount`, and the state directory gains a `pages/` subdirectory.
- **#1920.** The envelope gains two server-to-client frames: `page_changed`, carrying `id`, `op` (`created`, `saved`, `deleted`), `etag`, `by` and `client`; and `pages_reordered`, carrying `order`, `etags`, `by` and `client`.
- **#1925.** No change, but the boundary it drew is now used: page files take the compare-and-swap discipline it rejected for cells, on the document side of its own sentence.
- **#1931.** Its deferred Gridstack line is answered: pages are arranged on the panel and the library is still declined, for the reasons in the table above. Its record of the vendored copy as "Gridstack.js 12" is right; that copy is 12.6.0.
- **#2018.** Its deferred-list sentence "page arrangement by a vendored layout library, which returns only if pages are arranged on the panel rather than declared" is the contradiction the gap named; it should say that pages are arranged on the panel and the layout library is declined on its displacement model and its touch path, not on the arrangement question. Its "at most 32 steps are shown per grid view" is unchanged and is a legibility ceiling from #1927's arithmetic, not a lattice one.

## Not covered

- **Nothing has been rendered in a browser.** This workstation is headless with no browser (#1917), so the arrangement, the naming and the cache are state machines exercised under node with injected events, in the manner of #1927's `gesture.js`, and not a drag anybody has watched. The Gridstack rows are read from its source at a named tag, not from running it.
- **The frames are exercised through an injected hook, not over a socket.** `pages_routes.py` records every broadcast and the tests assert their content, order and absence, but nothing here has put a `page_changed` on a real WebSocket to a second browser. That is the end-to-end check named under Hardware-gated.
- **Default widget sizes per `kind` are not enumerated.** The bind picker drops a widget at "its default size for that `kind`", and #1916's manifest declares no size, so that table lives in the client. It is a small addition to #1931's widget set rather than to the contract, and it is not written here.
- **Undo of an arrangement** is not designed. The previous version is recoverable only if the page directory is kept in git, which #1916's "a file Simon can put in git" already anticipates; a `.bak` beside each page was considered and rejected as noise in a directory meant to be version-controlled.
- **Editing presence** — a panel warning that another panel is currently arranging the same page — is named as a cheap later addition (`hello` already carries the page) and is not designed; the 412 rule is what makes v1 safe without it.
- **Re-flowing a page across lattices** is deliberately not done: a page renders at its declared lattice, uniformly scaled to fit, with the surplus as margin, so no coordinate ever changes meaning. Re-flow — moving widgets to suit a different aspect ratio — is a later question and nothing here forecloses it.

- The felt size of a 60 px lattice snap and a 60 px key, and whether an arrange drag behaves with a palm resting on the glass, need the iiyama panel. #1998 already carries the 60 px cell as a hand check; if it moves the number, the lattice arithmetic above says what the panel's own lattice becomes and no saved page changes meaning.
- Whether `pointercancel` ever arrives during a slow arrange drag under `touch-action: none`, on Chromium under labwc and on Firefox and Chromium on Windows — the same open check #1927 already carries for its own gestures.
- Whether the labwc behaviour that hides squeekboard behind a fullscreen or kiosk Chromium window still holds on the Raspberry Pi OS version installed on the chosen host; the upstream issue is closed with no visible fix version and the Pi fork is moving, so it must be re-checked rather than assumed in either direction. It changes nothing above unless an OS keyboard is wanted.
- Whether a resting palm actually freezes and then ends a Gridstack arrange drag as `dd-touch.ts:37-39` implies — needed only if Gridstack is chosen over the bespoke engine.
- End-to-end confirmation that a page saved on one panel reaches a second panel's bar within one `page_changed` or `pages_reordered` frame, which needs two browsers on the LAN and the service on the headless server.
