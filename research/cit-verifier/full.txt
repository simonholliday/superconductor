Summary: This is the first-use-case design assembled from the sixteen delivered research points of the Superintendent specification and from the measurement rounds that complete them, offering Simon a short list and a recommendation for each of the seven decisions the specification named. The recommended shape is one Starlette-on-uvicorn systemd unit co-located with the music apps, serving a vendored buildless Preact page that holds a single same-origin WebSocket carrying JSON envelopes, with each app carrying its own adapter module on a daemon thread that dials the service and speaks the same frame vocabulary; the sequencer's grid is content in `composition.data` behind a new `StepGrid` helper, written by absolute sets applied in loop order, confirmed by an `ack` at application and a per-cycle event carrying the built version and the realised cells, with the playhead extrapolated client-side from per-beat anchors. The panel is drawn as DOM cells with a transform-moved playhead overlay and canvas layers for redrawn content, on a Raspberry Pi 5 kiosk conditional on a frame-time check, with a gesture set in which every two-contact gesture has a one-contact form; its pages are arranged on the glass by a bespoke snap-drag and tap-to-place on a 32-by-18 lattice of 60 px cells, saved through five conditional page routes whose stale writes are refused rather than overwritten, and named without any focusable input. The one coupling the research points had not weighed against each other is the thread-to-loop crossing, and it is recommended here as crossing per message with no batching device, no rate ceiling and no bulk fallback: the drain rule costs the clock nothing but puts the `ack`, and therefore the cell's face, a measured 243 to 251 ms behind the finger at the median, while the pending flag it was first written with never fires at a rate a hand produces and the burst that batching at the socket read would collapse is not one the envelope produces. It must still be answered together with the pending look. Grid patterns rebuild one pulse before their cycle, which is the only lookahead of the three whose rebuild pulse carries no note for the block to delay, and the rebuild-pulse budget of about 500 notes is a ceiling for the whole composition rather than per pattern. Subsequence needs no change to its clock, its rebuild or its scheduling: the package change is two new modules, four small engine edits and a dependency-floor fix, and five heavier items are named as needing a wave. Sixty-two questions and twenty-six hardware-gated checks are filed as Subroutine items and grouped here by what they block.

This document assembles the first-use-case design from the sixteen research points delivered against the Superintendent specification and from the measurement rounds that complete them. Each of the seven decisions the specification's closing section names gets a short list of at most three options, a trade-off table, and a recommendation with its reason. Simon chooses; nothing here is settled on his behalf. Every figure is from the filed research and carries its own caveats there; every measurement was taken on the development workstation unless the source document says otherwise, and is indicative.

The first use-case is a multi-page hardware-style sequencer controlling one Subsequence composition that plays hardware drum machines and synths over MIDI: a grid of steps by parts, a tap that lights at once and sounds on the next cycle, an algorithm and a human writing the same grid, a cell that shows when it is being played, and pages assembled from what the apps declare.

## How the surface is drawn

### Rendering technique

| | A. DOM cells in a CSS grid, playhead as one overlay moved by `transform`, canvas layers for continuously redrawn content (recommended) | B. Canvas 2D for everything | C. SVG per widget |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Incremental cost, 1024 cells, Pi 5-class throttle | 0.5 to 0.7 ms per frame with style and layout | 0.1 to 0.3 ms, the cheapest measured | 0.9 ms |
| A genuine every-cell change of 1024 cells, Pi 5-class | 8.6 ms, one dropped frame per snapshot; 22.8 ms at Pi 4 class | 3.9 ms of JavaScript, but raster-bound without a GPU (66 of 300 frames dropped) | 10.9 ms, about 25 percent dearer than DOM |
| Playhead move | 0.007 ms of Paint as a compositor-only `transform`, against 0.31 ms for class toggles | redraw two columns | attribute or transform on a rect |
| Touch hit-testing, per-contact pointers, labels, focus, ARIA | free | all by hand | free |
| What the worst case depends on | main-thread speed, which the throttle calibrates | the host's software raster path when acceleration is absent | main-thread speed |
| Fit for meters and waveform | canvas layer beside the DOM | native | poor for anything redrawn per frame |

WebGL is excluded: it is the cheapest technique with a working GPU and the only one that dropped frames at every throttle without one, and Chromium's acceleration on a Raspberry Pi cannot be relied on.

**Recommended: A.** Cost does not separate the three for the real workload — a column moves per step and a handful of cells change per beat, all under a millisecond per frame at Pi 5 class, and dirty-cell canvas is nominally cheapest. What decides it is that the DOM gives touch hit-testing, per-`pointerId` contacts, labels, focus and ARIA for nothing, that CSS grid places cells without computed coordinates, and that its worst case is bound to main-thread speed rather than to the host's raster path, which no measurement here can calibrate.

Rules that follow from the measurements and are part of the recommendation: never re-render the grid through the virtual DOM per frame (measured at six to seven times the imperative path); move the playhead by `transform` only; redraw canvases on data change, never on every animation frame; apply a snapshot or page switch as one batch and accept a one-frame hitch; give each grid `contain: paint` or its own compositing layer so a scattered edit repaints one grid rather than the page; keep glow effects off cells that change per step.

### Framework

| | Preact + htm, vendored as one standalone module (recommended) | Lit, vendored as one bundle | Vanilla ES modules and Custom Elements |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Build step | none | none (pre-built bundles avoid bare specifiers) | none |
| Size vendored | one file, 13,194 bytes uncompressed | about 5 KB compressed, larger uncompressed | nothing |
| Fit with pages assembled from declarations | components by hand | a control type maps onto one custom element, which is the Pages decision's own shape | as Lit, with more boilerplate |
| Precedent | the idiom of both prior codebases, which the brief says to learn from rather than extend | none here | none |
| Currency | Preact 10.29.8 current; htm 3.1.1 unreleased since 2022 | lit 3.3.3 current | n/a |
| Licence | MIT and Apache-2.0 | BSD-3 | n/a |

Svelte and Vue are excluded: Svelte needs a Node build, and Vue buildless loses single-file components and offers nothing over Preact at this size.

**Recommended: Preact + htm**, with the grid's hot path written imperatively through refs and class toggles rather than through the virtual DOM. Two rows of the table argue the other way and are weighed rather than dropped. Lit's custom-element-per-control-type is closer to the Pages decision's own shape, and pages assembled from declarations are the product's spine; against that, the mapping this design actually needs is from a `kind` to a render function, which is a dictionary lookup in either framework, and the grid — the one control whose cost was measured — is written imperatively in both, so the structural advantage applies to the cheap widgets and not to the expensive one. htm being unreleased since 2022 is the more real cost: it is a vendored dependency of an AGPL tree that must be able to supply the Corresponding Source, so it is vendored at a pinned version with its source URL and licence recorded beside it (the rule below), it is 700-odd lines of tagged-template parsing with no runtime surface of its own, and the fallback if it ever breaks is Preact's `h()` calls, which is a mechanical rewrite of the templates and touches nothing else. On the strength of that, Lit is the principled equal alternative rather than the loser, and the choice is genuinely Simon's (#1978); if he takes Lit, nothing else in this document moves.

### Widget sources

| | Bespoke widgets on DOM and canvas, borrowing the hardware conventions (recommended) | NexusUI vendored for the non-grid widgets | webaudio-controls for knobs and sliders |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carries the two-contact gestures | yes, by construction | no | no |
| Matches a flat glass surface | yes | its own look | skeuomorphic knob sprites |
| Waveform from Subsample's envelope | yes, the data is already the right shape | no consumer for it | no |
| Licence | ours | MIT stated in `package.json` only, no licence text (#1983) | Apache-2.0, code only; not the CC-BY sample images or the bundled Polymer polyfill |

**Recommended: write the widgets, borrow the conventions.** No library carries the gestures, none matches the look, and Subsample's 400-bin envelope suits none of them directly. For the waveform, serve `render_svg` inline first (zero browser code, proves the data path) and draw the envelope on canvas as the end state, when the widget must follow the theme or share coordinates with the playhead overlay.

The visual language is borrowed from Push, Launchpad, Digitakt and MPC: hue for state and luminance for velocity with a second cue beside brightness; a distinct moving playhead colour as a translucent overlay that preserves the cell underneath; steady for the page being edited and blinking for the page being played; tempo-synced pulsing for pending and armed; one global brightness control; a small palette of one identity colour per instrument plus a handful of state colours; square cells of one size in a fixed grid that pages rather than shrinks; transport and page controls outside the grid in a different shape from cells; at least 3:1 contrast between cell states and 4.5:1 for labels; flashing no faster than one flash per two beats; a light theme beside the dark one from the first version (#1981). At the panel's 0.249 mm pixel pitch, at most 32 steps are shown per grid view and longer patterns page, which is also what the hardware does. The working cell size is **60 px square**, whose arithmetic comes from a retailer specification sheet rather than from use and is exactly what #1998 exists to confirm by hand. That 60 px is also the page lattice's cell, so the panel's lattice is exactly 32 columns by 18 rows; the lattice places widgets and does not divide their insides, so the 32-step view is a legibility ceiling standing beside it rather than a lattice one.

Fonts are Inter with JetBrains Mono for numerals; icons are Lucide. All are vendored with their licence text; the fonts may be subsetted (only a modified IBM Plex may not keep the name "Plex"), and each minified vendored bundle records its upstream version and source URL so the Corresponding Source can be supplied.

## Where the UI is served from, and how the service relates to the apps

### Topology

| | C. One service co-located with the apps, serving the page and holding the browser's single socket (recommended; B is the same software elsewhere) | B. The same service on another host of the apps' network | D behind a reverse proxy. An adapter in each app, nginx or Caddy for one origin, the page aggregates |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Browser sockets | one | one | one per app, through one origin |
| Origins the page touches | one | one | one |
| CORS on fetches | none | none | none |
| One app down or restarting | the service reports it offline and retries; the browser socket is unaffected | as C | the page runs a reconnect machine per app; that app's panels blank |
| Second panel or a laptop | one socket; fan-out and snapshot cache once, in the service | as C | each app fans out to every panel from inside its own process |
| Where snapshot, fan-out, reconnect, page assembly and mock apps live | once, in one process, one concurrency model | as C | replicated into every app process; page assembly and mocks have nowhere but the browser |
| Inside the composition process | one outbound link | one outbound link | a listening server whose work grows with the number of panels |
| Pads to Subsample's virtual MIDI port | not a differentiator: under the adapter-only pad path settled below, the Subsample adapter opens the output inside Subsample's own process, so pads work identically in all three columns | as C | as C |
| Measured clock cost | median and mean unchanged; a sub-millisecond P99 in two of eighteen loaded runs that two repeats each failed to reproduce; only pinning service and sequencer to one core moved the distribution (P99 2.0 ms) | no CPU shared with the clock at all | socket I/O on the clock loop costs p99 about 1.1 ms; on a daemon thread, nothing measurable |
| Cost accepted | one more long-running process; a single point of failure for the page | a second machine that fails independently; a LAN hop per message | the proxy is the same single point of failure, plus N reconnect machines in the client |

The pad row is stated as a non-differentiator deliberately: the researcher's version counted it against option B, on the strength of the variant in which the service itself opens a mido output to the virtual port. That variant is excluded below, so the row cannot also be used to rank the topologies. Only if Simon reinstates it (#1973) does co-location earn a pad advantage, and then it is worth about 1 ms against a 3 to 23 ms output buffer.

A static page talking straight to app endpoints is excluded: browsers cannot speak UDP so OSC is not an endpoint, the REPL is loopback TCP, `web_ui.py` is frozen, and the shape becomes the in-app-adapter shape as soon as each app grows a browser-facing server.

**Recommended: C, with B as a deployment option of the same software.** It is the only shape in which every cross-cutting concern is written once in one process with one concurrency model rather than replicated into three, and it keeps what runs inside the composition to a single link whose work does not grow with the number of panels.

**Discovery is recommended with it, not settled by it.** The recommendation is a static URL for panels and registration by the apps: a browser cannot browse mDNS services, `.local` names are unreliable on Windows and in the Chromium snap, and registration adds no listening socket to any app, so neither the port map nor the inherited exposure grows. Which side dials is a filed question (#1950) and remains Simon's; the serving design keeps a `dial:` fallback in the configuration shape for an app that cannot dial out, and mDNS is a later addition nothing in this shape prevents. If Simon reverses the direction, three listening sockets, three bind-failure paths and a static address list in the service come back with it.

### The listen port and the exposure

The specification's port map reserves 9000, 9001 and 9002 (OSC), 5555 (the REPL), 8080 and 8765 (the legacy dashboard) and 9003 and 9004 (the Supervisor adapters), and requires Superintendent not to collide with any of them. The service's own port was left unnamed by every point (the serving design's configuration sketch writes it as a placeholder), so it is chosen here.

| | A. 8090 (recommended) | B. 8085 | C. 80 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Distance from the map | clear of every entry | clear of every entry | clear |
| What a person types | `http://<server>:8090/` | `http://<server>:8085/` | `http://<server>/` |
| Reads as | a web UI, beside the familiar 8080 | a web UI, beside the familiar 8080 | the machine's web service |
| Unit cost | none | none | binding below 1024 as `User=music` needs `AmbientCapabilities=CAP_NET_BIND_SERVICE` |
| Risk | a co-tenant that wants 8090, which is Confluence's default connector port | a co-tenant that wants 8085, which is Bamboo's default | collides with anything else that wants 80 on that box |

9005 is excluded rather than short-listed: it is IANA-assigned and reads as the fifth socket in the superseded Supervisor family, which is the wrong thing for the service that replaces them. 8000 is excluded because it is the most contended port on any development machine and it is Supervisor's own.

**Recommended: 8090**, configurable in one line of the service's YAML so nothing rests on it. The kiosk's URL lives in an autostart file rather than in anyone's fingers, so C's brevity buys little for a capability grant. This is a decision Simon should confirm and is filed as #2020, which carries the IANA registry entry for each candidate — 8090 is assigned as `opsmessaging` and 8085 is unassigned — the best-known de-facto claim on each, and 8085 as the equal alternative.

**The exposure, inherited and created.** Today every network surface in the three apps is opt-in and off by default. Once enabled, the ranking is: Subsequence's OSC receiver on UDP 9000 is the control door — it binds all interfaces by documented intent, and an unauthenticated sender can set tempo, mute or unmute any pattern, and write any `composition.data` key, which is where this design puts the grid; the Supervisor adapters on 9003 and 9004 are the read door, binding all interfaces and serving whole recordings directories with a CORS wildcard; Subsequence's REPL on loopback TCP 5555 is the only code-execution surface; Subsample's OSC receiver on loopback UDP 9002 accepts `/sample/import <path>`, an unauthenticated read of any readable file if it were ever rebound. Substation has no listening socket at all. Superintendent adds exactly one surface: its own listen port on the service, bound to the LAN interface because the browser and the apps both reach it. The apps gain none, because they dial out. Whether the composition on the rig enables OSC, and with which receive host, is #1955; passing `receive_host="127.0.0.1"` is a composition-file setting, not an engine change, because binding all interfaces is documented intent.

### The stack that runs it

| | Starlette on plain uvicorn plus websockets (recommended) | aiohttp | websockets plus a hand-written static handler |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Runtime distributions | 7 | 9, five with C extensions | 1, already in the tree |
| Warm import, peak RSS | 0.071 s, 27 MB | 0.141 s, 35 MB | 0.054 s, 24 MB |
| Static serving | ETag-aware `StaticFiles` | `web.static` | MIME, ETag, range and traversal by hand |
| Later HTTP, TLS, auth | ASGI: server and middleware interchangeable | aiohttp's own API | out of scope by the maintainers' own statement |
| Typing and house-style fit | fully typed, explicit route table | fully typed, explicit routes | plain functions |
| CPU under a sixteen-client load | 5 to 19 percent of one core | 2 to 5 percent, the lowest | 2 to 14 percent |
| Fan-out | one bounded queue per client, drained by its own task, is required work | the same | `broadcast()` never awaits, but has no backpressure |

FastAPI and Quart are excluded: FastAPI's pydantic validation and OpenAPI are unused by a WebSocket service and its footprint is the largest of the five; Quart is sixteen packages, its current release needs Python 3.13, and its decorator style pushes module-level state.

**Recommended: Starlette on plain uvicorn.** Starlette and aiohttp are both typed and complete. The CPU column is not close and should not be described as if it were: aiohttp measured 1.9 to 4.7 percent of one core, Starlette on plain uvicorn 9.2 and 18.9 percent in its two sixteen-client runs, and Starlette with the standard extra 4.3 to 5.6 percent — a factor of two to four in the worst comparison. The source attributes its two highest readings to other activity on the workstation, since the broadcast latency in those same runs was also the highest recorded, and none of it reached the clock in any run. Even taken at face value it is under a fifth of one core for sixteen clients, so it decides nothing unless the server is small, which is exactly what #1977 asks. On that footing Starlette wins on ASGI interchangeability for a later TLS or authenticated mode, the ETag-aware static handler, and the route table that reads in the house style; if the answer to #1977 is a small or busy server, aiohttp is the cheaper choice and costs only the ASGI option. Fan-out is one bounded queue per browser client drained by its own task, so a stalled Wi-Fi panel drops frames rather than delaying the others.

Deployment is one systemd unit (system or user, #1975) with `Type=notify` through a ten-line standard-library `sd_notify`, `Restart=on-failure`, `RestartSec=2`, `WatchdogSec`, no `After=` or `Requires=` on the music apps, cheap hardening, stdout to journald, and uvicorn's access log switched off explicitly with `access_log=False` and `log_config=None`. Configuration is one YAML file in the siblings' shape, found via `--config`, then the working directory, then XDG, then `/etc`, naming the service's listen address and port and the apps it expects; each app's side is one line, `superintendent: {enabled: true, url: ...}`, defaulting to loopback so the co-located case needs no edit anywhere. Packaging follows the siblings in every respect but one: setuptools-scm, a PEP 639 `AGPL-3.0-or-later` expression, the static UI and its licence files as package data, and no direct-URL dependency of any kind. The exception is `requires-python`: `>=3.12` is Subsample's floor but above Subsequence's and Substation's `>=3.10`, so it is not sibling precedent. Its grounds are Ubuntu 24.04's own interpreter and the websockets 17 floor of 3.11, and its consequence is that a 22.04 server's system Python is excluded and would need a venv on a newer interpreter, which is #1976.

Development mode runs a real Subsequence with no MIDI device and mocks only Subsample and Substation, because those need an audio device and an SDR; the mocks dial the service exactly as the real adapters do, so the service's own handling of a peer is exercised by a real client rather than by a fake shaped the way the service wished. That is not, on its own, the answer to Supervisor's sharpest lesson, and it should not be presented as one. What failed there was app-side: the real caller passes `player=_player_cell`, a one-element list, and `recorder_processor=None` (`subsample/cli.py:1750-1756`), so the adapter's `hasattr` probes were false forever and voices, queue depth and audio serving were dead in production while thirty-seven tests passed against fixtures that described the interface the adapter wanted. A service-side mock cannot catch that class of defect at all. So the practice carried is app-side and additional: **each app's own suite carries one test that constructs its Superintendent adapter exactly as that app's entry point constructs it** — `subsample/cli.py:1747-1759` and `substation/cli.py:240-252` are where the Supervisor hooks sit today and where the Superintendent hook goes — and asserts `json.dumps` on the real snapshot that adapter produces, which is also the boundary at which numpy scalars are caught (below).

**Rests on:** #1919, #1929, #1922, #1923, #2040.

## The protocol between browser and service, and between service and apps

### Browser to service

| | A. One WebSocket per page carrying JSON envelopes (recommended) | B. Server-Sent Events for push plus fetch POST for commands | C. A as above plus an unreliable-unordered WebRTC data channel for pad hits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Connections per page | one | one stream plus a request per command, under a six-per-origin cap on HTTP/1.1 | one plus a peer connection |
| Command round-trip, loopback | 0.8 to 1 ms median | 3.8 ms | as A |
| Ordering | one total order per connection | none between the command channel and the stream | ordered channel plus an unordered one |
| Reconnect | client code: backoff plus `hello` with the version map | free for the stream via `Last-Event-ID`; none needed for commands | client code plus an ICE restart |
| Liveness | client `ping` every 2 s, 6 s stale timer on inbound frames | the stream needs a heartbeat event; fetch has no timeout of its own | as A |
| Python side | `websockets`, already a dependency on both sides | any HTTP server | aiortc, with unverified mDNS candidate handling |

WebTransport is excluded for v1: it is HTTPS-only, needs an ECDSA certificate under fourteen days hash-pinned into the page, and has no Python framework integration. It is the right WAN transport later, and the envelope carries over unchanged.

**Recommended: A.** It is the only short-listed option with a single total order per connection, `websockets` is already a dependency on both sides, it measured a quarter of fetch's command cost, and its two weaknesses — no built-in reconnect and no protocol Ping exposed to the page — are about forty lines of client code. Liveness must be client code whichever option is taken: a SIGSTOPped server was invisible to headless Chromium 151 and Firefox 153 for over 30 s on the socket, and a fetch POST to the same stalled server hung until a 30 s client abort, while a killed server took about 0.6 s.

The envelope is small UTF-8 JSON, one object per frame, with `permessage-deflate` off. Client to server: `hello` (contract version, client id, page, the last `ver` per app, and **an optional `token` field ignored in v1**), `set` (`seq`, `path`, absolute `v`), `fire`, `ping`. Server to client: `manifest`, `snapshot` (per app, stamped with `ver`), `ack`, `nack`, `changed` (with `by` and, when `by` is `panel`, `client`), `event`, `telemetry`, `pong`, `app` presence, and the two page frames `page_changed` and `pages_reordered` that carry a save, a create, a delete or a reorder to every other panel. The token field costs nothing now and is one of the four zero-cost choices that keep a remote mode open — the other three being a single origin for page, socket and media, relative URLs with no CORS wildcard, and the apps on loopback with the service the only network face — and it is the one whose omission would force a protocol change later rather than a check. JSON stays for v1: MessagePack and CBOR are 1.6 to 4.8 times smaller but encode plus decode is microseconds at this scale in both Python and V8. Binary frames are reserved for bulk payloads with a one-byte kind tag.

The lifecycle is fixed: a full `manifest` and `snapshot` on every connect regardless of the version map, because a 128-cell snapshot is under 1 KB and 12 microseconds to encode and always-snapshot has no edge cases; a 2 s client `ping` whose echoed timestamp also gives the playhead its clock offset; a 6 s stale timer reset by any inbound frame; exponential-backoff reconnect from 250 ms to a 5 s cap with jitter; server-side `ping_interval` and `ping_timeout` at 5 s; pending `set` frames keyed by client id and `seq` re-sent after a reconnect's snapshot, while `fire` is never re-sent because a late pad hit is worse than a lost one; a pending `set` unacknowledged for 5 s reverts on the panel. The client suspends its ping while hidden and forces a fresh `hello` on `visibilitychange` and `pageshow`.

### Service to apps

| | A. A per-app adapter module dialling the service over one WebSocket (recommended) | B. The same module over a stdlib TCP socket speaking newline-delimited JSON | C. An embedded WebSocket server in each app, behind a reverse proxy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Codec in the service | one, shared with the browser | two framings and a hand-rolled keepalive | one |
| Snapshot on connect | `manifest` then `snapshot`, re-sent on every reconnect | as A | on accept |
| New listening sockets in the apps | none | none | one per app |
| Keepalive, framing, close | from the library | hand-rolled | from the library |
| Measured cost to Subsequence's clock at 50 Hz | on the loop p99 1.22 ms; per-message from a thread p99 1.10 ms; queued on the thread and drained at the sequencer's own hooks, p99 0.002 ms against a 0.002 ms baseline | per-message from a thread p99 1.05 ms; with the drain, identical to A because the app-side half is the same code | threaded server p99 0.003 ms, but fan-out and snapshot caches move into every app process |
| New dependencies | `websockets` as an optional extra in Subsample and Substation; Subsequence's floor rises to 13.0 | none | as A |

OSC over UDP is excluded as the standard shape: it has no connection on which to hang a snapshot, cannot see an absent peer, does not fit a typed declaration, and reusing Subsequence's receiver would put Superintendent behind the LAN-open user OSC door. It stays the user-facing surface it is. MIDI into Subsample's virtual port is a complement carried as a `fire` frame, not an alternative.

**Recommended: A, as one module per package** (`subsequence/superintendent.py`, `subsample/superintendent.py`, `substation/superintendent.py`), each on its own daemon thread with its own loop, opening a single outbound WebSocket, sending `manifest` and `snapshot` on connect, and speaking the browser's frame vocabulary so the service has one codec. It is the only option giving the service one codec, one connection direction and one failure story across all three apps while adding no listening socket to any of them; its measured clock cost with the drain is nil where every per-message shape costs about 1.1 ms at p99; and it is the shape the recommended topology's registration assumed. The cause of that 1.1 ms is CPython's epoll selector rounding every selector timeout up to a whole millisecond, so a mid-sleep wake-up overshoots the clock's 1 ms spin margin (`sequencer.py:465`) — an engine-side lever, not a transport property (#1974).

The app-side rule is part of the shape and differs per app because their concurrency models differ:

- **Subsequence.** Inbound `set` frames go into a bounded queue and are drained on the loop by a `reschedule_pulse` listener, awaited immediately before the rebuild that consumes the grid (`subsequence/sequencer.py:1804`), and by a `beat` listener for patterns not rescheduling this pulse. **When that drain happens is the one open coupling in this design and is short-listed under the grid below**; the transport shape is the same whichever crossing rule is chosen, because both use the adapter's own queue. Verbs that must not wait a beat go through the existing thread-safe paths at the cost of one wake-up each. Outbound frames are built on the loop in a `pattern_reschedule` listener (`sequencer.py:1829`) and appended to one ordered log the link thread drains. Nothing runs in the pulse's own processing.
- **Subsample.** The module subscribes to `app_events` and the player's events and copies under its own lock; a `fire` becomes note-on and note-off on a mido output the module opens to the process's own virtual port, which keeps the rtmidi callback thread the only MIDI handler (`subsample/player.py:4444-4450`; the lock-ordering rule is written at `player.py:7-29`, whose operative half — "never acquire either while holding any of the others" — is on the last of those lines). It holds the set of pads it has pressed and releases them on link loss, because a `gated` or `loop` voice sounds until a note-off or CC 123.
- **Substation.** The module registers synchronous handlers for the four recording and state events, which land on the scanner loop by one route or the other, and for the two per-slice events, which run on the executor thread and where it swaps references rather than locking; a band request is marshalled onto the scanner loop with `call_soon_threadsafe`.

**Every adapter sanitises numpy at the JSON boundary.** This is Supervisor's most easily repeated defect and the one this design walks straight back into: Substation's SNR and activity values are `numpy.float64` and `numpy.bool_`, Subsample's preview block is a 400-bin int8 envelope, Substation's recording envelope is computed the same way, and `json.dumps` has never accepted `numpy.bool_`, `numpy.int64` or `numpy.float32` — `numpy.float64` passes only because it subclasses `float`, which is why the failure looks intermittent and why Supervisor's README wrongly blamed a Python release for it. Each adapter sanitises at the source or installs a `json.dumps(default=)` hook, never by sniffing type names, and the constructor test above asserts `json.dumps` on the real snapshot so the boundary is covered by a test rather than by care.

V1 ships three per-app modules pinned by one protocol specification with executable test vectors run by each app's own suite, rather than a fourth shared package; extraction into a package once the three exist and converge is mechanical (#1972).

## The control declaration and the message contract

| | A. A typed control tree of groups and typed leaves (recommended) | B. W3C Thing Description verbatim | C. OSCQuery verbatim |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Covers a drum grid, a melodic row, a pad bank, a fader, a band selector, a waveform, a meter | yes, in one schema | grids have no shape and a cell no address; flat name-keyed maps need a grouping convention | no record type for a cell |
| Null and records on the wire | JSON carries both | JSON carries both | OSC's nil is a nonstandard tag receivers need not recognise |
| Says when a write lands | `applies`: `immediate`, `next_cycle`, `restart` | nothing equivalent | nothing equivalent |
| Widget hint separate from value type | yes | no | partly, through `EXTENDED_TYPE` |
| Carriers | one, whichever the transport points choose | `forms` bind each interaction to a protocol, duplicating the transport decisions | pairs HTTP for the tree with OSC for values |
| Mandatory ceremony | none | `@context`, `security`, `securityDefinitions` | none |
| Standing | new schema to own and version | W3C Recommendation | a proposal |

A widget manifest in Supervisor's or TouchOSC's shape is excluded: it bakes layout into the app, which breaks the decision that pages are free-form and user-defined, and it has no value model. MIDI 2.0 Property Exchange verbatim is excluded because the apps are not MIDI devices to the panel; its `typeHint` and `priority` are borrowed.

**Recommended: A.** It covers every worked example in one schema, JSON carries null and records natively, it is transport-neutral so an OSC mapping or a Thing Description export can be added later without redesign, and it borrows vocabulary from OSCQuery (attributes), the Thing Description (the property, action and event split, DataSchema names) and Property Exchange (`typeHint`, `priority`), adding only `applies`, which nothing in the prior art has and the apps genuinely need: a step tap lands next cycle, a pad hit is immediate, a Substation band change is a restart.

A leaf carries `id` (the name exactly as the app knows it, OSC-legal), `path`, `kind` (`toggle`, `trigger`, `number`, `select`, `grid`, `text`, `meter`, `waveform`, `list`, `group`), `value`, `access`, `range`, `default`, `unit`, `hint`, `applies`, `priority`, `tags`, `description`, and `rate_hz` on meters. A `grid` leaf adds a `shape` with its own ordered row list, `cols`, `page`, and a `cell` schema; grid rows are the grid state's own rows, merely labelled through the pattern's `drum_note_map`. Sub-paths address a row and a cell; a cell is a record with `on` plus declared future fields. Messages are explicit `set` and `fire` on paths with a client `seq`, answered by `ack` with the app's `ver`; there is no toggle verb, because `set` is idempotent and a replayed toggle inverts a cell. Namespace change travels as `path_added` and `path_removed`, so a pattern registered in a live reload appears and an unregistered one leaves a page's tile unbound rather than wrong. Three independent versions — `contract`, `app_version`, `page_contract` — plus ignore-unknowns keep an old page working against a new app release.

**The message-contract half of this decision is short-listed, but the tables live with the grid**, because the options only make sense beside the rebuild model: absolute set against per-cell compare-and-set against multi-value merge, and per-cycle `cycle` event against per-cell build confirmation against `ack` alone, are both tabled under "Conflict policy, confirmation, the algorithm's hand and the pending look" below. Nothing here is settled prose that has not been short-listed somewhere.

This assembly makes six amendments to the contract as filed. Four are additions or harmonisations that reconciling two points requires and that the assembly states flatly; two change what an app may declare or what v1 carries and are therefore Simon's, not the assembly's:

- **Simon's.** `changed` leaves at application rather than at the rebuild, and a new per-cycle `cycle` event is the build confirmation — a revision of the contract's semantics, filed as #1967. And the drum-grid example's `v1` list becomes `["on", "vel"]` with `vel` ranged 1 to 127 rather than 0 to 127, because a velocity of zero is a note-off in MIDI and in the sampler; this fixes what v1 cells carry and should be confirmed rather than assumed.
- **The assembly's.** `client` is added to `changed` beside `by`. The declared `gesture` field is dropped in favour of optional `levels` on a cell field and an optional velocity mode with a fixed value on a pad trigger. An `envelope` value type, a `list` kind with a `records` value and a `mode` field on `trigger` are added for the later pages. And the stored cell's velocity key, written `v` in the grid design and `vel` in the contract, is harmonised on `vel`: the grid design left the wire name to the contract explicitly, so this is that decision being taken rather than a change to either.

### Where pages live, and how one is arranged

| | A. Edited on the panel, saved as files on the service, cached in the browser (recommended) | B. Browser `localStorage` only | C. A hand-edited service config file only |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Drag-to-arrange on glass | yes, on the lattice below | yes | no |
| Survives another browser, cleared site data, a reimage | yes | no | yes |
| Two panels share a page | yes, under the conditional save below | no | yes |
| Version-controllable beside the composition | yes | no | yes |
| Cost | five page routes on the service, a 105-line arrangement engine and a naming path in the client | none | editing a grid layout by hand is the wrong tool |

**Recommended: A**, with the service file authoritative and the browser cache holding the last-open page and what the page bar needs to draw. Candidate pages are derived from the declarations by one rule — one candidate per top-level group, groups merged by declared instrument, and one candidate per distinct tag across apps — and offered when a page is added; what is shown is always the saved page. Candidates need no route of their own, because that rule is a pure function of the manifests the client already holds. Where the page files sit is Simon's (#1948); the recommendation is `$STATE_DIRECTORY/pages/` where systemd provides it and `$XDG_STATE_HOME/superintendent/pages/` otherwise, and only the root moves if he answers otherwise.

**The lattice.** A page places its widgets on a lattice of 60 px cells — the working cell size above — which on the 1920 by 1080 panel is exactly 32 columns by 18 rows with nothing left over on either axis, one cell being 14.94 mm on the glass and so above every finger-target figure the multi-touch work records. `x`, `y`, `w`, `h` are integers in that page's own lattice, and the page declares its lattice, so if #1998 moves the 60 px the lattice moves with it and no saved page changes meaning: a page renders at its declared lattice, uniformly scaled to fit with the surplus as margin, and is re-authored at the panel's cell the next time somebody arranges it and saves, which is a visible act rather than a silent reinterpretation. Sixty is the only plausible cell size that tiles both axes exactly, which is a reason to prefer it and not a reason to depend on it. A viewport smaller than the declared lattice — the laptop, or the desktop's second monitor — scales uniformly by `min(1, vw / (cols * cell), vh / (rows * cell))`, capped at 1 so a larger surface leaves surplus rather than magnifying the page, and says so when its cell falls under the enhanced touch target. Whether the transport and page bar takes a lattice row from the page area or floats over it fixes the lattice every page document is authored against, and is Simon's (#2039).

**The lattice places widgets; it does not divide their insides.** A widget's interior is its own layout at its own pixel sizes, which is why the 32-step grid view above and a 32-column lattice do not collide: a full-width grid widget spans all 32 lattice columns, or 1920 px, and inside it an 80 px label gutter leaves 32 step cells of 57.5 px — inside the 45-to-57 px band the legibility arithmetic gives, and not equal to the 60 px lattice cell. Sixteen steps on the same span are 115 px. The 32-step ceiling is therefore a legibility ceiling and never a lattice one. A sixteen-step by eight-voice drum grid wants about seventeen lattice columns and nine rows.

**How a page is arranged.** By a bespoke snap-drag written for this lattice — 105 non-comment lines — with tap-to-place on the same engine, so arranging works on a mouse-only or single-contact host as well as under a finger. A drag begins at the first `pointermove` past 10 px of slop with no time threshold; overlap is refused rather than resolved, the ghost shows the landing invalid and the widget snaps back, and nothing but the dragged widget ever moves. Tap-to-place is the same `legal()` and the same commit with no drag at all: tapping a widget latches it and tapping a lattice cell moves its top-left corner there. Corner resize takes the same clamp and the same refusal.

Edit mode is a latched control in the page bar, outside the grid where the visual language already puts transport and page controls, so no cell gesture can reach it. On entry a scrim covers each widget's body, so an arrange contact is never the cell recogniser's event target, with a mode flag as the second belt; in edit mode there is no hold gesture at all, so the 400 ms hold has nothing to arm against, and in play mode the arranger is not attached to anything. Adding and removing widgets is inside edit mode and is a tap on chrome rather than a gesture: a `+` opens the bind picker, which is the manifest tree the client already holds, and a tapped leaf drops a widget of its default size at the first lattice position `legal()` accepts. State stays live while arranging so the layout can be judged in motion, and no `set` or `fire` leaves the panel while the mode is latched. Leaving edit mode abandons an in-flight drag and saves; a 1 s debounce after the last commit also saves, so a crash mid-arrangement loses at most a second.

**A vendored layout library is declined, and not on the arrangement question.** At the released Gridstack tag its touch path opens by ignoring any event carrying a second contact, which on a ten-point panel means a resting palm anywhere freezes the drag; it calls `releasePointerCapture` on every non-mouse pointer, which is the opposite of the implicit-capture discipline the cell recogniser rests on; and its engine resolves every overlap by moving something — the dragged widget past the collider, or the collider down — with no branch that refuses a placement. The last of those is in the layout engine rather than the drag layer, so it survives the variant that keeps the library for its model and drives it with a bespoke drag, and it is the wrong behaviour on an instrument, where knowing without looking that the pad bank is bottom-right is the whole point of a fixed layout. Against that the library offers responsive re-flow, breakpoints, inertia and auto-compaction that one fixed-resolution panel does not want, and a touch-drag delay that is on its unreleased tree and would arrive with the next release, so vendoring one version and never updating would become an obligation to keep. A general-purpose gesture library is excluded rather than short-listed: it does not claim grid layout, collision resolution or serialising positions, so choosing it is choosing the bespoke option and paying a dependency for the pointer plumbing, which was never the hard part. Declaring pages in a file with no on-panel arrangement is the honest fallback if the browser work is deferred — the candidate rule and the page routes are needed either way and the file format does not move — but it does not deliver the reason this page model was chosen.

**How a page is saved.** Five routes on the service, declared ahead of the static mount: `GET /api/pages` for the listing, `GET /api/pages/{page_id}` for the document with a strong `ETag`, one `PUT /api/pages/{page_id}` whose `If-None-Match: *` creates and whose `If-Match` replaces, `DELETE /api/pages/{page_id}` under the same rule, and `POST /api/pages/order` carrying the whole page strip's order in one conditional request, because `order` lives inside each document and moving one page rewrites several — every named page's ETag must still be current or nothing at all is written. A request with no precondition is refused 428, a stale one 412 carrying the current ETag, a body over 256 KB 413, and an id the rule refuses 400. The ETag is a hash of the exact bytes on disk, so an unchanged re-save changes nothing for anyone; the whole read-compare-write is held under one `asyncio.Lock` per page id, which on a single-process single-loop service is the entirety of the mutual exclusion; and the write is a temporary file in the same directory replaced into place, so no reader ever sees a truncated page and a crash mid-save leaves the previous page intact.

This is deliberately the opposite discipline from the one the grid takes, on the boundary the state-sync design itself drew: rejecting stale writes is right for a document, while for an instrument the later hand is right by definition. A page arrangement is minutes of hand work that a stale write would erase, so the loser is told rather than overwritten and is offered the two honest choices — take theirs, or re-send the same document against the ETag the refusal named. Which of the two the panel offers is Simon's (#2038). A file that will not parse, or whose name the id rule refuses, is listed with the reason and its real filename rather than skipped, because a page file is something Simon may hand-edit and put in git.

Two frames carry the result to the other panels: `page_changed`, with an `op` of `created`, `saved` or `deleted`, and `pages_reordered`. The `op` is what makes a create or a delete made on one panel reach another's page bar at all, since a page nobody is showing is otherwise routed to nobody. The browser's half is the other end of the same loop: it draws the cache first and reconciles second, so nothing on the panel waits for the LAN; it draws the cached page and says so plainly when the service is unreachable, because a cached ETag is a precondition the service will judge rather than a claim about the service; it carries a precondition on every save it can build, so 428 is unreachable from the panel; and a refusal keeps the work, marks it unsaved and remembers the ETag it named, never retrying by itself.

Nobody is authenticated, so any browser that can reach the service may `PUT` or `DELETE` any page. That is inside the specification's security decision and the inherited-exposure record, but it is a destructive surface this design creates rather than inherits, and it is named here so it is not discovered when a remote mode is designed (#1954).

**Naming a page with no keyboard.** Generated names are the default, so the common case types nothing: tapping a candidate creates the page at once with an id derived from the app and group names, uniquified, and the candidate's own label. The id is derived once and never changes, so a rename is a label change that moves no file and invalidates no cache. Renaming uses a page-drawn key grid — the keys are cells, the field is a plain element, nothing is focusable — which stays inside the suppression set that forbids focusable text inputs, and the same state machine takes hardware keystrokes wherever a keyboard is attached, since a keystroke needs no focusable element. An operating-system keyboard is not used: reaching one on the recommended kiosk host costs either the fullscreen kiosk that host decision chose or a forked keyboard build, and buys a surface the page cannot control and that may cover the field being typed into. The `--kiosk` recommendation therefore stands unchanged. Whether renaming on the panel is wanted in v1 at all is Simon's (#2037); if it is not, the key grid is not written and nothing else here moves.

**The page document's version moves to `page_contract: "1.1.0"`**, gaining `order` and `lattice` and stating the units of `x`, `y`, `w`, `h`. A document still at `1.0.0` is read with the lattice defaulted to the panel's and sorts after every page that declares an `order` — a stated read rule rather than a default invented in the handler, and the first exercise of the versioning the contract carries for exactly this.

Leave the file alone (recommended); the alternatives are a nested `superintendent:` section, which breaks its flat-integer rule and drifts silently because neither loader validates it, or flat integer sections such as `voice_order:`, which is a contortion because colour and label are not integers. ## How the grid lives in Subsequence, and what confirms a tap

The rebuild model settles the shape before any design. Every pattern function is re-run once per cycle by a rebuild that first empties `steps`, `cc_events`, `osc_events` and `raw_note_events` (`subsequence/composition.py:5995-5998`), and `Pattern.steps` is a sparse dict keyed by pulse offset (`subsequence/pattern.py:147`), so the engine never stores a grid: it realises one. The grid is therefore **content the builder reads**, exactly like the literal step lists in today's composition files, and the panel is one more writer of that content. Subsequence stays authoritative because only the rebuild turns content into sound.

### Where the grid state lives

| | A. A `StepGrid` helper object under a Superintendent key in `composition.data` (recommended) | B. A plain dict under the same key | C. Per-pattern tweaks plus a new `p.set_param()` builder verb |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Package change | one new helper module; no engine file touched | none | one builder verb, because `p.param()` is read-only today so the algorithm cannot write |
| Validation of out-of-grid, non-integer and out-of-range writes | at write time, once | none; a step index at or past the grid silently plays in the next cycle's time and a non-integer index places a note between steps | none |
| Off-loop writes | applies directly when already on the loop, marshals only when not | a dict assignment is atomic, but growing a nested dict while the builder iterates it raises | whole-value replacement sidesteps the race by construction |
| Version for sync | a monotonic counter, plus `built_ver` and `applied` stamped at build | none | diff of the tweaks |
| Both hands write through one API | yes | yes | only with the new verb |
| Survives live reload | yes, seeded get-or-create | yes, same condition | lost on unregister and re-add |
| Persistence across restart | free: JSON in, JSON out | free | not free |

An engine-native grid object is excluded: the helper gives the same ergonomics with no engine change, and the sugar over it is a two-line wrapper if it is ever wanted.

**Recommended: A.** The storage is identical to B — the same key in `composition.data` (`composition.py:1454`), so a composition can start with a plain dict and migrate — but the four things every composition would otherwise reimplement live once: bounds, type and range validation; the loop-affinity rule; an edit counter for sync; and the mapping from cells to the builder's own verbs. Where the helper sits, and whether its key may be named for its consumer, is Simon's (#1942).

### When a panel write crosses onto the composition loop

This decision was not put to Simon by any single point, because each of the two points that touch it assumed the other's answer. The grid and state-sync designs assumed the helper marshals each panel write onto the loop promptly and priced the acknowledgement at "a few milliseconds"; the adapter design measured that call at about 1.1 ms at p99 on the clock and moved the crossing into a queue drained at the sequencer's own hooks, at no measurable cost. Both are right about what they measured, and the combination has a consequence neither states: `beat` fires once every 24 pulses (`sequencer.py:1427`, `pulses_per_beat` set from `MIDI_QUARTER_NOTE` = 24 at `sequencer.py:357`) and `reschedule_pulse` once per pattern cycle (`sequencer.py:1804`), so under the drain rule a tap waits **up to one beat** to be applied — and the `ack` that moves the cell's face leaves at application. That wait is now a measurement rather than arithmetic: a median of 243 to 251 ms and a maximum of 497 to 500 ms at 120 BPM, within a millisecond of the beat that bounds it.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tap to `ack`, and therefore to the face | up to one beat: measured median 243 to 251 ms and maximum 497 to 500 ms at 120 BPM, 1000 ms at 60 BPM, 333 ms at 180 BPM | a few milliseconds, of which the crossing itself is 0.03 to 0.15 ms at the median and 0.16 to 0.46 ms at p99; the rest is network | as B, with no loop involved at all |
| Against Nielsen's 0.1 s instantaneous threshold | over it by up to ten times | inside it | inside it |
| Measured clock cost, isolated taps at 5 Hz | p99 0.002 to 0.019 ms against a 0.002 ms baseline | p99 0.55 to 0.73 ms, 4 to 7 pulses over 1 ms in 1536 | no pulse over 1 ms, and no wake-up at all |
| Measured clock cost, a held paint gesture at 25 Hz | as above | p99 1.03 to 1.30 ms, 18 to 48 pulses over 1 ms in 1536 across four blocks; the dearest traffic a panel produces | p99 0.046 to 0.106 ms, at most one late pulse |

**Recommended: B, crossing per message, with no batching device, no rate ceiling and no bulk fallback.** The reason for B over A is the specification's own decided table: a step tap's transport latency is irrelevant because it is heard on the next cycle by design, *and the cell should light at once*. Under the recommended pending look the face is the server's value, so the `ack` is the face, and a face half a beat behind the finger on average fails that requirement outright. What B costs instead is a clock cost no listener can hear: at a human tap rate four to seven pulses in 1536 arrive about a millisecond late against a 20.833 ms pulse, and at the paint rate 18 to 48 do. The worst single pulse in any run measured for this decision was 1.69 ms, against a clock whose own mean jitter is about 3 µs. The jitter benchmark's own rating scale is not the right instrument for judging that, because it bands the mean and never a percentile; the honest figures are the late-pulse count and the worst pulse.

**No device sits inside B, and the three that were weighed are all dropped.** A pending flag that schedules a drain only when none is outstanding was measured never to fire at any rate a hand or a browser produces — exactly one message per wake-up and a largest batch of one at 5, 25, 50 and 200 Hz, and seven wake-ups saved in 14,087 at 500 Hz — because the loop services a drain in about 0.2 ms and the next frame is milliseconds away; on a burst it catches about eight frames, not the burst. Batching at the socket read does collapse a burst into one crossing, seventeen crossings for seventeen bursts of 128 frames and eight for eight of 1024, but at every rate a hand or a browser produces it *is* per-message crossing, and the burst it collapses is not one the envelope produces: under the contract in force a `set` addresses a leaf, a grid row or a grid cell, so a row clear, a fill, a paste and a preset load are each one frame; a page-load seed is an outbound `snapshot` rather than inbound traffic; and an algorithm's bulk write never leaves the process. The one inbound burst left is the reconnect re-send of pending sets, which per-message crossing carries for one late pulse of about 1.2 ms in 1536. Buying that pulse would cost a second code path, a socket probe whose natural form silently never batches at all, a bound that has to be chosen and defended, a drain rule to keep #1925's ordering, an in-burst crossing tail of 4.6 to 20 ms at p99 (part of which is the probe rather than the batching, and the probe written correctly is slower again), and a second panel's tap held behind somebody else's preset load for a median of 9.8 ms at 1024 frames against 0.03 ms per message. A timed batching window is excluded rather than short-listed: frames at the paint rate are 40 ms apart, so a window wide enough to merge anything a hand produces adds exactly that to the path the column exists to shorten. And a rate ceiling is dropped because it buys nothing rather than because it would do harm: a runaway client at 200 or 500 Hz costs the clock nothing measurable, and a ceiling low enough to catch a paint gesture would sit inside the gesture. The whole overload story is #1928's bounded queue, which drops and counts when full, with a `nack` and a close on sustained overrun.

**What would reopen it, and to what.** If #2008's re-measurement on the headless server shows a wake-up costing materially more there, the device to add is a read batch **capped at 64 frames** — measured at 126 crossings for 7182 messages with a second panel's tap held 0.8 ms rather than 9.8 — and never an uncapped read or the pending flag. Two rules belong with it: the read batch is capped, because the queue behind it can only drain once the batch ends, so an uncapped batch in front of a bounded queue truncates one edit in the middle instead of shedding at the edge; and the apply drain is bounded by whole frames and never split inside one, continuing with `call_soon`, because a `reschedule_pulse` listener can run between continuations and a rebuild must never consume a half-applied row. Under the recommended shape neither rule is needed. The device sits entirely inside one adapter module and touches no contract, so it can be added the day a measurement asks for it; what #1928's inbound rule becomes if Simon answers B is #2030, and whether the envelope gains a multi-path `sets` frame is #2031, recommended out of v1 because the bulk edits it was proposed for are already one frame each. C is the escape if the real server ever makes B unaffordable — it is the only column free at any arrival rate, and the engine already writes `composition.data` from a non-asyncio thread (`sequencer.py:770-772`, the write at `803`) — at the price of re-opening #1925.

**This decision and the pending look must be answered together**, and the two coherent pairings are these: B with the ring (the recommendation of both this section and the next, where nothing on a face is ever speculative and the face follows within milliseconds), or A with the optimistic face (where the cell flips locally on the finger and the server's value corrects it half a beat later, which is what makes A survivable). A with the ring is the combination to avoid, and it is the combination the points as filed would have produced. #1968 should be answered in that light; the crossing rule itself is filed as #2021, which carries this short list and its figures.

### Timing, and the composition-file setting it implies

A tap lands in the next cycle only if it is applied before that cycle's rebuild, which fires one `reschedule_lookahead` before the cycle starts (default one beat, `subsequence/pattern.py:105`). Measured offline: tap to cycle start is one lookahead at best and one cycle plus a lookahead at worst. With the default lookahead that bad window is a quarter of a one-bar pattern, so a tap on the cell under the playhead during the last beat is heard again after two cycles instead of one — 4 s instead of 2 s at 120 BPM. The lookahead is therefore a choice, and it is one Simon is asked to accept as a composition-file convention (#1943), so it is short-listed rather than stated.

The second thing the lookahead decides is which pulse pays for the rebuild. The loop rebuilds the patterns due on a pulse and only then dispatches that pulse's own events (`sequencer.py:1484-1485`), so the whole rebuild block runs *in front of* that pulse's MIDI and delays it by the whole block, on every cycle rather than only when the block overruns. The lookahead cannot buy the rebuild more time — it has exactly one pulse interval to complete in whatever the setting — so what it chooses is the pulse, and with it whether that pulse carries any note to delay.

| Effect on the jitter benchmark's pooled figure | median and p95 at baseline, p99 up to 0.419 ms, at most two pulses over 1 ms in 1536 | the same, indistinguishable | the same, indistinguishable |
| Floor for a step-count change | one step | four sixteenth steps | one step |
| Engine change | none; a composition-file setting | none | none |
| Safe against the form and harmony clocks | yes: those use the maximum of one beat and the pattern lookaheads, so a smaller pattern lookahead reads state that has already advanced | yes | yes |

Zero is excluded: it puts the rebuild on the cycle's own first pulse, where the block lands squarely on the downbeat's own MIDI.

**Recommended: A, on two independent grounds.** The first is the tap window. The second is the displacement: A is the only setting of the three whose rebuild pulse carries nothing to delay, so the block — 0.35 ms or 6.19 ms, it makes no difference — displaces no note at all. That is arithmetic rather than luck. The rebuild fires at `cycle_start_pulse + length_pulses - lookahead_pulses` (`sequencer.py:1789-1790`, `1821`), so a one-pulse lookahead lands on the cycle's last pulse; steps land on multiples of the step interval, which divides the cycle length because a cycle is a whole number of steps, and for it to divide the cycle length minus one as well it would have to divide 1 — a step grid of one pulse per step, 96 steps to a 4/4 bar. On any coarser grid the pulse before a cycle starts carries no note-on, and with the builder's default gate no note-off either. A quarter beat is exactly one sixteenth step and a beat exactly four, so B and C both land on a step and both pay, pushing that step's note-ons late by the whole block on every cycle — two to six milliseconds of flam on a beat boundary at 512 notes. What A pays instead lands on the downbeat and is small: a rebuild that holds the loop two to three milliseconds into its pulse makes the next wake-up late, and under A that next pulse is the cycle's own downbeat, whose first send was late by at most 0.84 ms and on most cycles not at all.

Two caveats belong with the recommendation. It holds for any step grid coarser than one pulse per step, which is every grid the first use-case contemplates; a pattern gated short enough to place a note-off on the cycle's last pulse would put a note-off, not a note-on, into the displaced position, and a late note-off is far less audible. And A does not make a large grid safe, because the headroom is identical under all three settings.

**The headroom, and what it is a budget for.** The constraint is that the block fit inside one pulse: 20.833 ms at 120 BPM, 13.889 ms at 180. A 16 by 8 drum grid is at most 128 notes and consumed 3 to 16 percent of a pulse, a margin of six to thirty times. Headroom runs out between 512 and 1024 notes per rebuild pulse at 120 BPM on the development workstation — about 800 at the CPU frequency floor, about 540 at 180 BPM — and patterns sharing a length and a lookahead are rebuilt one after another in a single pass (`sequencer.py:1799-1833`), so their costs add on that one pulse: four identical fully-filled 16 by 8 grids behaved like the single 512-note grid. The ceiling is therefore a budget for the whole composition's rebuild pulse rather than per pattern, which is what page assembly has to respect, and it is put to Simon as #2035; staggering lengths or lookaheads across patterns spreads the block over different pulses and costs nothing to do. When a block does exceed a pulse the overrun appears as exactly one late pulse and does not accumulate (`sequencer.py:1507-1535`).

**The largest single term is neither the grid nor the lookahead: it is the host's CPU frequency state.** The same rebuild is five to six times dearer with the core near its 800 MHz floor than at full clock, and a sequencer that spins for at most 1 ms of every 20.833 ms pulse is exactly the workload a power-saving P-state policy parks near the floor. Setting the governor to `performance` on the machine that runs the composition removes that factor from every rebuild. It is a change to the host rather than to any package, and it is asked as #2034.

**The engine option this opens is not needed by the recommended shape.** The displacement exists because the loop rebuilds before it dispatches, so swapping the two calls would remove it under every lookahead, every step grid and every gate — safely whenever the lookahead is at least one pulse, because the rebuilt cycle then starts after the current pulse, and unsafely at a lookahead of zero, which the scheduling code accepts today. Under A there is nothing to fix, so v1 leaves the order as it is. The swap is the lever if a pattern ever needs a larger lookahead, if a one-pulse step grid ever exists, or if the drain point moves; it interacts with the crossing rule above, because it moves when a write drained at `reschedule_pulse` is applied relative to that pulse's own MIDI. It is filed as #2036.

The remaining latency is the cycle itself, which is the instrument's nature and the specification accepts it.

### Conflict policy, confirmation, the algorithm's hand and the pending look

Every write reaches the grid on the composition's asyncio loop — panel writes because the adapter drains them there, algorithm writes because they happen inside the builder — so writes have a total order without any clock, and "last writer" means "applied later on the loop".

| Sub-decision | Short list | Recommended | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Conflict policy | two: loop-order last-writer-wins, and per-cell compare-and-set with a `stale` refusal | **loop-order last-writer-wins** | Absolute sets already remove the hazard compare-and-set protects against, so it buys only a refusal the human overrides by tapping again, and it would turn the reconnect re-send into a stream of refusals. For an instrument the later hand is right by definition, which is the rule Ableton Link chose for tempo. Multi-value merge is excluded rather than short-listed: there is one authority, and a resolution interface on a step sequencer is absurd. |
| Confirmation carrier | three: a per-cycle `cycle` event carrying `built_ver`, a `state` word, the cycle index, the start pulse and the full realised cell map; a per-cell build confirmation; none, `ack` plus the playhead | **the per-cycle `cycle` event** | Only a complete map shows what will actually sound, covering probability, mute, the energy gate, an ephemeral algorithm's notes and a REPL poke. A per-cell confirmation cannot express gating and never settles a ring under mute, because a muted pattern skips the builder while its confirmation hook still fires. |
| The algorithm's hand | three: ephemeral contribution beside `apply()`; persistent intent unconstrained; persistent with a one-cycle `touched` yield guard | **ephemeral as the default idiom**, persistent when an edit is meant to last, the guard one call away | Under the ephemeral shape the human's intent is never erased and state stays late-bound; the algorithm's hand still appears on the panel at the rebuild, as ghost and hollow cells in the realised map rather than as changed faces. All three coexist in one composition and the panel draws them without knowing which is in use (#1965). |
| What a pending tap looks like | three: the face is always the server's value and a ring is the human's unconfirmed request; an optimistic face plus a ring; an optimistic face alone | **face is the server's value, ring is the request**, conditional on the crossing rule above | Nothing drawn on a face is speculative, so every panel agrees on every face; the ring appears on the finger landing and the face follows on `ack`, which is a few milliseconds on a LAN **under the recommended crossing rule and up to one beat without it**. The optimistic face is the right answer if Simon keeps the drain rule, and is the fallback anyway if the ring proves illegible on the panel (#1999) or when a WAN mode arrives (#1968). |

A tap therefore passes three moments and the protocol names all three: **applied** (`ack` to the sender and `changed` to every panel, the instant the helper writes the intent on the loop — a few milliseconds after the finger under the recommended crossing rule, up to one beat under the drain rule), **built** (the `cycle` event, within a pulse of the rebuild and one lookahead before the cycle starts), and **sounding** (the playhead's overlay). `built_ver` is stamped inside the helper's `apply()` and falls back to a version recorded at `reschedule_pulse` when the builder is skipped, because a muted or energy-gated pattern never reaches `apply()` while `pattern_reschedule` still fires. All frames enter one ordered outbound log appended on the loop at the moment of application and drained by the link thread. That log is load-bearing rather than belt-and-braces: `pattern_reschedule` is not awaited but spawned (`sequencer.py:1829`, against the awaited `reschedule_pulse` at `1804`), so a panel write already in the loop's ready queue when the pulse handler runs executes after the rebuild and before the listener, and without the log its `ack` would leave the process ahead of the algorithm's earlier `changed`.

### The playhead

| | B. Anchored client-side extrapolation (recommended) | A. Server-pushed `beat`, highlight on arrival | C. A new per-pulse server event |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What travels | the existing `beat` frame plus `pulse`, the wall time taken in the listener, and the current tempo; per-pattern `cycle_start_pulse` and `length_pulses` on snapshot and each reschedule | the existing `beat` frame | 24 to 72 frames per second per client |
| Engine change | none for v1 | none | one spawned emit, measured free on the clock |
| Error, wired LAN, computed for presentation time | 5.2 to 23.6 ms behind the MIDI send | 5.5 to 24.1 ms | as A plus pulse quantisation |
| Error with a 50 to 80 ms Wi-Fi tail | unaffected: a late anchor still says when its pulse happened | up to about 102 ms, past the 45 ms sound-leading detectability threshold | as A |
| Tempo ramp, Ableton Link, external MIDI clock | works: the anchor is a measured time | drifts within a beat | exact to a pulse |
| Hidden tab, reload, reconnect | resumes on the next frame, position being a function of absolute time | resumes on the next beat | resumes on the next pulse |
| Traffic | 1 to 3 frames per second per client | the same | 24 times that |

The legacy dashboard's 100 ms polling is excluded outright: its position error alone is 0.4 to 1.2 steps at 60 to 180 BPM.

**Recommended: B.** It is A with three numbers added to a frame that already exists, and it removes the network from the playhead position entirely, which is the only way a Wi-Fi panel keeps the column inside the detectability threshold. Constraints that come with it: the tempo slope must come from the current BPM or from the interval between anchors and never from the seconds-per-pulse value, which external MIDI clock never updates; the position is computed for the frame's presentation time, not for callback time; the highlight steps rather than slides, because vision is the looser rhythmic sense and a sliding bar shows the frame quantisation itself; and the sounding flash rides the realised view of the coming cycle with a fallback to the intent grid when that frame is late (#1964). The listener that takes the anchor runs 0.06 to 0.37 ms after that pulse's note-on left the sequencer, so its timestamp is the MIDI send time to within half a millisecond. The drum machine's own MIDI-to-sound latency is rig-specific and belongs in the composition file or the panel's settings, never in the engine (#1962).

The gesture design's own lettering ran to E; the three honest options are relettered here and nothing is silently dropped — the two omitted from the source's list were a per-row velocity lane and a modifier key, both of which fold into A's strip or B's switch rather than standing as alternatives.

**Recommended: A**, with an optional latched accent switch on the page and the tap acting on press rather than toggling on release. This departs from the specification's decided wording "hold-one-cell-and-tap-another for accents" in that the second tap lands on a strip level rather than on another cell; the literal reading is B and is on the short list for Simon (#1969).

Chords need no recogniser at all: under Pointer Events each contact is its own `pointerId`, so a chord is several pointers down at once wherever the host delivers concurrent contacts. The only gesture needing recognition is the hold, at a 400 ms threshold taken from the shorter of the two platform norms.

The v1 set, every row with a one-contact form so the page never asks whether multi-touch exists: a press on an empty cell sends its `set` the moment the finger lands rather than on release, and the panel draws the pending ring in the same frame, so something changes under the finger inside the 69 ms tapping just-noticeable difference (#1970) — the cell's *face* follows the `ack`, which is why the crossing rule and the pending look above have to be chosen together and why the recommended pair is the one that makes this sentence true of the face as well; a quick press on a set cell clears it on release; a 400 ms hold opens a per-cell velocity strip without clearing the cell, worked by a second finger or, after a visible 3 s latch, by the same finger; cells pressed while the strip is open join the selection so one level edits several; a pad is one voice per contact, down firing on and up firing off, with fixed velocity by default because glass carries no velocity; faders and knobs move any number at once, each capturing its own pointer. Painting along a row is on the list but is Simon's to admit or defer (#1971); a hold-to-lock gesture is reserved (#1966). Capability is observed from the pointers actually delivered rather than asked of the browser's touch-point count, which Firefox has reported wrongly in both directions, and the observation changes nothing but a status glyph.

The two cases have thresholds an order of magnitude apart. A step tap's visual acknowledgement has 64 to 69 ms before a typical user notices it. A pad hit has 10 ms with 1 ms of jitter as the musical standard, 20 ms already rated worse than 10 by percussionists, and 24.5 ms as the edge of "Fair" for drums.

The life of one tap, which is the design's centre, under the recommended crossing rule:

```mermaid
sequenceDiagram
  participant F as finger
  participant P as page
  participant S as service
  participant L as adapter link thread
  participant C as composition loop
  F->>P: pointerdown on an empty cell
  P->>P: draw a dim ring at once, no network
  P->>S: set seq, path, {on: true}
  S->>L: relay the set
  L->>L: cross onto the loop for this frame, no batching
  C->>C: drain the queue, StepGrid applies the intent, ver advances
  C->>S: ack (seq, ver) and changed (by panel, client)
  S->>P: ack to the sender, changed to every panel
  P->>P: face becomes the server's value, ring goes bright
  Note over C: at the next rebuild the builder runs, apply() stamps built_ver
  C->>S: cycle event: built_ver, state, cycle, start_pulse, realised cells
  S->>P: cycle to every panel
  P->>P: ring clears; face solid, hollow or ghost
  Note over P: the playhead reaches the column, extrapolated from the last beat anchor
```

Under the drain rule the two middle steps move: the frame waits for the next `reschedule_pulse` or `beat`, half a beat on average and one at worst, and the ring holds the finger alone until then.

The components in prose:

**The panel** runs a browser and nothing else. It loads one page from the service over plain HTTP on a LAN address, opens one same-origin WebSocket derived from `location` so it inherits whatever scheme it was loaded under, and holds no state that matters: a client id and a `seq` counter in page memory, a pending map of unacknowledged sets, the last-open page in browser storage as a cache. It draws grid cells as DOM elements in a CSS grid, moves the playhead as one absolutely positioned overlay by `transform` alone, and puts continuously redrawn content on canvas layers. It computes the playhead's position for each frame's presentation time from the last beat anchor and its own clock offset, and it never waits for a network frame to acknowledge a finger.

**The service** is one Starlette application on uvicorn, one systemd unit, one process, one LAN listen port. It serves the static page and its vendored assets with ETags, holds one WebSocket per browser with a bounded outbound queue drained by that client's own task, holds one WebSocket per app that the apps themselves opened, owns the per-app `ver` and stamps it on what it relays, keeps the manifest and last snapshot per app so a fresh panel needs no app round trip, keeps the (client, seq) idempotency map, stores page files under a `pages/` subdirectory of its state directory and serves them through five conditional `/api/pages` routes declared ahead of the static mount, and knows which apps to expect so an absent one shows as offline rather than unknown. It owns no musical state, no MIDI stack, no process management and no access to the apps' files.

**Each app carries one adapter module**, enabled by configuration or by an opt-in call beside the existing OSC and REPL calls, degrading to a warning when the package or the service is absent. It runs on its own daemon thread with its own loop, dials one address, sends its manifest and snapshot on connect, retries with capped backoff, validates every inbound frame before queueing it, sanitises numpy at the JSON boundary, and never blocks or raises into its host. In Subsequence it also builds outbound frames on the loop in the reschedule listener and appends them to one ordered log the link thread drains, so a panel write that lands between a rebuild and its spawned listener cannot leave the process out of order.

**Failure is contained at every boundary.** The service absent: each app runs exactly as it does today and reconnects when it returns. An app absent: the service reports it and the page greys that section; a command sent in the gap is refused with a reason. The service dead: systemd restarts it, and a loaded page reconnects with backoff and resyncs by snapshot. A bad grid message: refused at the helper's boundary rather than logged every cycle under a misleading message; a bad rebuild costs one pattern one silent cycle and never the clock (`subsequence/sequencer.py:1810-1828`). A stalled panel: its own queue drops frames and the other clients are unaffected. A stalled server: the client's 6 s stale timer closes the socket, because nothing else notices.

## Engine changes proposed for Subsequence

Nothing here touches the clock, the rebuild or the scheduling. Two of the items are new modules that touch no engine file; four are small edits inside engine files; one is a dependency floor. That is the honest scope, and it is what #1465 and #611 make Simon's to judge.

### Already possible, no package change

- The grid as content the builder reads from `composition.data` (`composition.py:1454`), re-read at every rebuild (`composition.py:5995-5998`).
- An algorithm writing the same structure, or contributing ephemerally beside the helper's apply.
- Changing the step count from the panel through the builder calling `set_length` and passing the step count per call; measured to take effect at the very next cycle boundary with the events already in the heap untouched (#1946).
- Snapshot on connect through `live_info()` (`composition.py:3868`) plus the pattern's realised steps read on the loop.
- Change notification at `pattern_reschedule` (`sequencer.py:1829`), spawned as a task straight after the rebuild, so a listener there sees the algorithm's writes at the moment they take effect; and `reschedule_pulse` (`sequencer.py:1804`), which is awaited, as the commit point and one drain point.
- Declaring `reschedule_lookahead=1/24` on grid patterns: a composition-file setting (`pattern.py:105`).
- Marshalling a panel write onto the loop, if the recommended crossing rule is taken: the thread-safe path exists and is what the live reloader and `trigger()` already use; nothing new is needed in the engine for it.
- Containment for every bad message measured except an out-of-grid or non-integer step index, which the helper must refuse.
- Row naming through the pattern's drum note map, with the realised view preferring each note's own origin name.
- The playhead anchor, from the public pulse count, pulses per beat and current tempo read in a `beat` listener.
- An optional audition of a freshly set cell in the current cycle through the composition's thread-safe one-shot trigger (#1945).
- Apps reachable only on loopback, by the composition passing a loopback receive host to its OSC call (#1955) — a composition-file setting, not an engine change, because binding all interfaces is documented intent.

### Small: two new modules, four engine edits and a floor

- **`subsequence/helpers/grid.py` with `StepGrid`** — a new module; no engine file touched. It validates at write time, applies directly when already on the loop and marshals only when not, keeps a monotonic version, stamps `built_ver`, `built_cycle` and an applied flag in `apply()`, keeps a `touched` set cleared at apply with a convenience that skips touched cells, takes a `skip` argument for the ephemeral idiom, holds the single ordered outbound log, reserves a `lock` cell field, and maps cells to the builder's own verbs. Its seed is get-or-create so a live reload does not overwrite panel edits. Its placement, and whether its key may be named for its consumer, is #1942.
- **`subsequence/superintendent.py`**, the adapter module — a new module; no engine file touched. Enabled by an opt-in call in the same pattern as the existing OSC and REPL calls, carrying the walk that builds the manifest and never a rig value.
- **Engine edit: public read access to what the adapter would otherwise read privately.** `cycle_start_pulse`, the grid, the drum note map, the device and the mirrors in each `live_info()` pattern entry; read-only properties for the muted and energy-gated flags; a maximum-device-latency property (the value exists at `sequencer.py:419`) so the highlight can be offset by it (#1962). **This bullet is wider than the question filed for it.** #1963 asks about three exposures only — the pulse in the beat payload, `cycle_start_pulse` in `live_info()`, and the maximum-device-latency property — of which the first is its own bullet below. The `live_info()` extensions for the grid, the drum note map, the device and the mirrors come from the grid design's small list, and the read-only muted and energy-gated properties come from the state-sync design's revisions. Answering #1963 as filed decides only part of what is proposed here, so either #1963 is widened to the full set or the remainder is put to Simon separately; that is filed as #2019. That spike also narrows the remainder against the code: `device` and `mirrors` are already public attributes of `Pattern` (`pattern.py:132-133`), reachable through the public `Composition.running_patterns` property (`composition.py:1800-1803`); `muted` is already in every `live_info()` pattern entry (`composition.py:3901`); and `cycle_start_pulse` already arrives as the second argument of every `pattern_reschedule` event (`sequencer.py:1829`). The facts with no public route at all are the pattern's grid, its drum note map, the energy-gate flag and the maximum device latency.
- **Engine edit: the pulse in the `beat` payload** beside the beat-in-bar, which today carries the beat alone (`sequencer.py:1427`); this removes the adapter's inference from the pulse count and the catch-up ambiguity that inference has after a stall.
- **Engine edit: a runtime grid setter** so the builder's own grid value and the many algorithmic verbs that default to it follow a changed step count, and the display draws the right grid. This is a writer, not a read-only exposure, and is outside #1963's scope entirely.
- **Engine edit: pattern registered and unregistered events, and a source-reloaded event**, so the namespace-change frames need no polling; until they exist the adapter diffs the running patterns on `bar`.
- **Dependency floor: raise `websockets` from `>=12.0` to `>=13.0`** (`pyproject.toml:23`), which the legacy dashboard's own import of the new asyncio server already needs and which nothing in the tree satisfies by declaration today. The service pins 14 or later in its own environment.
- **Candidate, to be checked against the design-intent convention first**: warn once for a step index at or past the grid, or a non-integer index, both of which silently misplace a note today. Negative wrap is documented and stays.
- **Optional**: a flag recording that a build failed after apply, so a silent cycle can be reported as failed rather than as empty; and a sugar accessor over the helper.

### Needs a wave — five items

- Cutting a cycle already in the event heap, per pattern. Heap entries carry a device and a channel but no pattern identity, so a per-pattern removal needs event tagging, heap surgery and active-note bookkeeping. A purge keyed by device and channel is possible today and is right whenever a pattern owns its channel, which the first use-case does, but wrong when two patterns share one, which this design allows; it is a stopgap, not the answer.
- Making the grid a first-class runtime property that the display and the algorithmic verbs follow without an explicit setter.
- A per-pulse or per-step engine event. Measured to cost the clock nothing on the engine side, and it buys nothing over the anchored playhead; it is named so that nobody proposes it again without a reason.
- Dispatching a pulse's own events before rebuilding the patterns due on it (`sequencer.py:1484-1485`). It removes the rebuild's displacement of that pulse's MIDI under every lookahead, every step grid and every gate, and it is safe whenever the lookahead is at least one pulse and unsafe at zero. The recommended one-pulse lookahead needs none of it, and it moves when a write drained at `reschedule_pulse` is applied relative to that pulse's own MIDI, so it interacts with the crossing rule (#2036).
- Choosing a different asyncio selector, or widening the clock's spin margin (`sequencer.py:465`), if per-message immediacy on the clock loop is ever wanted; measured to remove the millisecond entirely, and an engine change rather than a transport choice (#1974). It is the C column of the crossing short list above, so the recommended shape does not need it and would benefit from it.

### What the two neighbours need, for completeness

Subsample needs a note event beside its single control-change event (`subsample/player.py:5055`, the only event emit in that file), a program event, one trigger declared per note of a multi-note assignment, and its adapter module; nothing else, because its virtual port is already the trigger path (`player.py:4444-4450`) and every sample already carries the envelope the waveform page draws. Substation needs a public stop wrapping the sentinel it already queues cleanly (`substation/scanner.py:941-969`), about a dozen lines looping around the single scanner construction in its runner (`substation/cli.py:150-167`), and its adapter module; that is the whole band switch, and it keeps the link up and the service out of process management (#1938, #1984).

## Not covered

- **The tap-to-face figure over a real path.** The crossing decision above is argued from workstation measurements of the clock cost, of the crossing itself and of the drain interval, all of which are now measurements rather than arithmetic, but the end-to-end figure a player would feel — finger to face across the real LAN, with the service and the composition on the headless server — needs a second host and is #2002. Only the inbound half of the crossing is timed anywhere: the loop handing an `ack` back to the link thread and out of the socket is not instrumented in any block.
- **The socket work a batching device would save.** The wake-up counts are measured and the clock cost is a tie between every crossing shape; nothing counts socket reads, frame assemblies or JSON parses on the link thread, which is the one ground for read-batching these measurements do not touch. The in-burst crossing figures also carry the prototype's own read probe — about three fifths of the batched median at a 1024-frame burst — and the probe written the correct way is slower again, so those figures belong in the argument as a range.
- **A real `StepGrid.apply()` in place of the dict write.** Every crossing row applies one dict write. #2008 and #2009 carry that, and it is what decides how large a drain may safely be if a device is ever added.
- **The mechanism of the wake-up lateness after a rebuild.** A rebuild that holds the loop two to three milliseconds into its pulse makes the next wake-up 0.6 to 1.5 ms late in thirty of thirty-one samples, where the epoll rounding model predicts none in all 126; it is not monotone in the block cost, the frequency or the margin, and it was not isolated. It matters here as at most 0.84 ms on the downbeat under the recommended lookahead, and a faster rebuild would move most rebuilds out of the band (#2034).
- **What the displacement sounds like.** The dispatch lateness is measured, not judged. Under the recommended one-pulse lookahead nothing is displaced, so the question only arises under a larger one.
- **The real MIDI write.** No port was opened in any of the rebuild measurements, so the rtmidi write cost falls on the dispatch pulse and adds to the displacement rather than to the block; a rig-accurate total for a 512-note cycle is not in these figures.
- **Nothing has been rendered in a browser.** The arrangement engine, the naming state machine and the browser's cache reconciliation are exercised as state machines under node with injected events, not as a drag anybody has watched, and no `page_changed` has been put on a real socket to a second browser. Default widget sizes per `kind`, which the bind picker needs, are in the client and are not enumerated anywhere yet.
- **A builder that raised after calling apply.** It is indistinguishable in the `cycle` event from a builder that placed nothing; naming the cause needs the optional build-failed flag above.

## What remains out of scope

The specification's own exclusions stand: nothing is built beyond measurement prototypes, the legacy dashboard is untouched, the composition wave's refusals are not reopened, security hardening is out for v1, and nothing specific to Simon's particular drum machines and synths appears anywhere but his composition file.

Persistence of grid state across a Subsequence restart falls out for free and is therefore noted rather than designed: the grid is JSON-able content under one key, so a composition file can seed it get-or-create from a literal or a sidecar and the adapter can dump it.

The following are deferred by the research, named here so nothing is silently dropped and nothing is proposed again without a reason: an unreliable side-channel for pad hits, which buys nothing on a wired LAN and costs signalling and connection setup; WebTransport, until a remote mode with HTTPS exists, at which point the envelope carries over unchanged; a binary codec, which saves bytes and no measurable time at this scale; a replay ring buffer for resync, which the version in the opening frame already allows and which no client would change to use; a per-cell lock and the gesture that would arm it, reserved as a cell field so the wire shape does not move when it arrives; a fixed-offset pad path, which would roughly double a 6 ms median to remove about 2 ms of jitter and only becomes attractive if the panel confirms the extra Chromium frame; Substation's in-place retune, which saves only the device reopen and the calibration; a shared adapter package, until the three modules exist and converge; a vendored layout library for page arrangement, declined on its single-contact touch path and on a displacement model its layout engine has no branch to refuse, rather than on the arrangement question, since pages are arranged on the panel; a batching device on the adapter's inbound path, which returns only if the headless server's own measurement asks for it and then as a read capped at 64 frames; mDNS discovery, which no browser can consume and which nothing in the recommended shape prevents adding later; routing two instances of one app; a scale-degree lane for melodic rows; velocity in the realised map; and an OSCQuery or Thing Description export of the manifest, which the contract's transport-neutrality leaves open.
