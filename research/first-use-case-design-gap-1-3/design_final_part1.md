**Question.** The first-use-case assembly names three questions in prose that no `needs_input` task carries — the thread-to-loop crossing rule, the scope of the read-only engine exposures, and the service's listen port — so how are they filed, and what short list does each carry?

They are filed as #2021 (the crossing rule), #2019 (the exposure scope) and #2020 (the listen port), each linked to #2018 and to the documents it rests on, and #2018 is amended everywhere it said they were unfiled, so its question list names all fifty-three. Checking the three against the code, against the registry and on the clock changed all of them, and the corrections are on the spikes.

## Why these three and not the other fifty

The fifty filed spikes (#1942 to #1991) were each raised by a research point and filed with it. These three were raised by the assembly itself: when two points that had never been read against each other turned out to disagree, when an engine-change bullet turned out wider than the question filed for it, and when a number no point had been asked for had to be written down. Nothing in the process filed them. #2018, as it stood before this point amended it, said so under Not covered: "The crossing rule, the scope of #1963, and the listen port are decisions this assembly raises rather than inherits, so no `needs_input` spike carries them yet. Each is named in its section and in the question list; they should be filed before the list is put in front of Simon."

The first of them is not a loose end. #2018 calls the crossing "the one open coupling in this design", and it gates the grid decision, one of the seven the specification requires the assembly to put to Simon. The seven-decision list was incomplete at the point where it matters most.

## The crossing rule

**What it decides.** Whether the Subsequence adapter, holding an inbound `set` frame on its own daemon thread, crosses onto the composition's asyncio loop at once, waits for the sequencer's own hooks, or applies the value on its own thread and never crosses.

**Why it was never asked.** Each of the two points that touch it assumed the other's answer. The state-sync design prices tap-to-`ack` at a few milliseconds on the assumption that the helper marshals each panel write onto the loop promptly (#1925); the adapter concurrency finding measured that call at about 1.1 ms at p99 on the clock and moved the crossing into a queue drained at the sequencer's hooks at no measurable cost (#1926). Both are right about what they measured, and the combination was never priced. `beat` fires once every 24 pulses (`subsequence/sequencer.py:1427`, with `pulses_per_beat` set from `MIDI_QUARTER_NOTE` at `sequencer.py:357`) and `reschedule_pulse` once per pattern cycle (`sequencer.py:1804`), so under the drain rule a tap waits up to one beat to be applied, and the `ack` that moves the cell's face leaves at application: 500 ms at 120 BPM, 1000 ms at 60 BPM, 333 ms at 180 BPM.

**What does not move.** Tap to sound is the same in every column. The `reschedule_pulse` drain is awaited immediately before the rebuild that consumes the grid — `await self.events.emit_async("reschedule_pulse", pulse, patterns)` at `sequencer.py:1804`, ahead of the rebuild loop that begins `for scheduled_pattern in to_reschedule:` at `sequencer.py:1808` and ends with the `pattern_reschedule` emit at `sequencer.py:1829` — so a queued tap still makes the cycle it was going to make. What the rule moves is the face.

**What the short list changed.** #2018 gives three columns: drain at the hooks, wake the loop "coalescing a burst into one wake-up", and the second plus the engine-side selector lever. The coalescing does not exist (measured below), and a fourth shape the engine itself already uses was missing: the link thread can replace the value on its own thread and never cross at all. The list below is the corrected one. The selector lever is not a column of its own, because it modifies B rather than competing with it.

**Recommended: B, with A retained as the automatic fallback** for anything that is not a human's tap — a page-load seed, an algorithm's bulk write, a held paint gesture beyond a rate ceiling — #1974 named as the lever if the real headless server is worse than the workstation, and C named as the escape that buys A's clock cost with B's face at the price of re-opening #1925. The reason is the specification's own decided table: a step tap's transport latency is irrelevant because it is heard on the next cycle by design, *and the cell should light at once*. Under the recommended pending look (#1925, #1968) the face is the server's value, so the `ack` is the face, and a face up to one beat behind the finger fails that outright. Nielsen's 0.1 s is the threshold both #1925 and this document score against: "0.1 second is about the limit for having the user feel that the system is reacting instantaneously, meaning that no special feedback is necessary except to display the result" (Jakob Nielsen, 1993, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/).

**Where B's few milliseconds come from.** #1925 prices tap to `ack` from #1920's measurement: a 0.8 to 1 ms loopback browser round-trip plus about 1 ms of wire, with the service-to-app hop and the marshal onto the loop untimed and loopback again, so of the same order. The end-to-end figure across a real LAN is #2002 and is unmeasured. The budget is therefore an estimate with one measured term, not a measurement.

**Why C is on the list and not recommended.** The engine already writes `composition.data` from a non-asyncio thread: the rtmidi callback applies CC input mappings inline on its own thread, on the stated ground that "Single dict writes are safe from a non-asyncio thread under CPython's GIL" (`subsequence/sequencer.py:770-772`, the write at `sequencer.py:803`), and the Supervisor lesson the specification carries is atomic reference swaps rather than locks across thread boundaries. #1914's own state-location table records the hazard and the fix: growing a nested dict while the builder iterates it raises `RuntimeError`, and "whole-grid replacement avoids the iteration race by construction". So C is real, and it is the only column that costs the clock nothing while lighting the cell at once. What it costs is the invariant #1925 is built on — "Every write that reaches the grid is applied on the composition's asyncio loop... Writes therefore have a total order without any clock, and 'last writer' means 'applied later on the loop'". Off the loop there is no such order against the algorithm's writes inside the rebuild, the version counter and the one ordered outbound log are appended from a second thread, and the equality test that decides whether a set produces a `changed` no longer runs where the current value is authoritative. Taking C means re-opening #1925's version rules, which is a larger change than the millisecond it saves; it is named as the escape if the real server makes B unaffordable, because A gives up the face and C does not.

**This must be answered with #1968.** The pairings are these. B with the ring: nothing on a face is speculative and the face follows within milliseconds; this is the recommendation of both. A with the optimistic face: the cell flips locally on the finger and the server's value corrects it up to a beat later, which is what makes A survivable. A with an `ack` sent by the service on receipt rather than at engine application: the ring clears in milliseconds against a value the service has accepted and every panel is told, so panel agreement survives and the clock is untouched, at the price of a second version axis — the service's accepted version beside the helper's applied version — which is machinery #1925 deliberately does without, and of an `ack` that no longer means what #1925 defines it to mean. A with the ring as #1925 defines it is the combination to avoid, and it is the combination the points as filed would have produced. #1968's own body still says of the two looks that "both are inside 0.1 s on the LAN", which is true only under B or C; a comment on #1968 records that.
