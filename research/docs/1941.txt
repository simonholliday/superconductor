  finding · active · #latency · #research · superintendent

Summary: The literature puts the just-noticeable latency for direct-touch tapping at 64 to 69 ms (Jota 2013, Deber 2015) and for a percussive instrument at 10 ms with 1 ms of jitter as the standard (Wessel and Wright 2002), with 20 ms already rated worse than 10 ms by percussionists (Jack et al. 2016 and 2018) and 20 to 30 ms as the detection threshold for a continuous instrument (Mäki-Patola 2004). Measured in headless Chromium 151 and Firefox 153 on the workstation, a pointer event reaches its handler in 0 to 2 ms with a mouse or with Firefox's synthetic touch, but in Chromium's synthetic touch path it arrives 0.5 to 18 ms later, tracking the frame boundary, so the class change is committed one frame after that: event to commit is 17 to 34 ms for Chromium touch against 1 to 18 ms for mouse and 0 to 20 ms for Firefox touch. The WebSocket `fire` leaves the page 0.1 ms after the class change, reaches an aiohttp service in 0.45 ms and a second-process adapter on loopback in 1.0 ms median, 1.2 ms p99, 1.4 ms max. Adding the sibling findings (0.24 ms into Subsample's virtual port, under 1 ms dispatch, an output buffer of 2.7 to 23 ms per period with PortAudio running several periods) gives a pad path of 2 to 17 ms from the browser's event timestamp to the DAC at 128 frames on one host, 5 to 26 at 256 and 10 to 43 at 512, about 1 ms more per LAN hop, and 4 to 32 at 128 frames with Chromium's two-frame touch dispatch. Finger to sound is that plus the panel's own touch-down latency, which is unmeasured and bounded only by Microsoft's 35 ms guideline: at a 15 ms allowance the pad path is about 20 ms at the median and about 34 ms at the maximum with 128 frames. A step tap's visual acknowledgement is the browser term plus one vsync and 5 ms of pixel response, 22 to 42 ms from browser event to glass before the panel's touch-down latency and before the panel's uncosted display input lag, inside the 69 ms threshold as long as touch-down stays under about 25 ms with a one-frame browser and about 13 ms with Chromium's two-frame path. Whether the 10 ms musical standard is reachable turns entirely on the panel's touch-down latency, the one term this pass could not measure. The transport is not where either budget goes: a grid tap's audible effect is the cycle (#1915), a Substation band switch is about 2 s plus a device reopen or about 16 s with RTL-SDR calibration (#1938, #1939), and over a WAN pads are impractical while the grid, whose local mark is drawn without the network, is unaffected.

**Question.** What are the latency budgets for the two cases the brief names, visual acknowledgement of a step tap and a pad hit heard from the hardware, and what does each hop actually cost?

Three consequences. The two cases have thresholds an order of magnitude apart: a tap's visual echo has about 64 to 69 ms before a typical user notices it, while a pad has 10 ms before a percussionist does and 20 ms before the instrument is rated worse. Jitter is judged more harshly than latency in the audio case, by roughly a factor of ten, and the browser's frame quantisation is a jitter source of up to one frame on any path that waits for a frame. And the audio threshold is decided by a term nobody here has measured: the software path from the browser's event to the sampler's DAC has a median of 6 ms at 128 frames, inside the 10 ms bound, so what puts the standard out of reach is the panel's own touch scan, for which the only figures available are Microsoft's requirements on Windows touchscreens (touch-down at most 35 ms when active in the current guideline, 25 ms in the Windows 8.1 certification kit) rather than a measurement of this panel. The working assumption below is that 10 ms finger-to-sound is not reachable; it is an assumption resting on one hardware-gated measurement, not a proof.

## Method

Measured on the development workstation (Intel i7-9700K, Linux 6.8.0-138, Python 3.13.11, load average 0.3 to 0.5) on 2026-09-03 with research prototypes under the scratchpad `latency/` directory, not house style: `page.html` is a pad and a 128-cell grid with a `pointerdown` handler that changes a class, sends a `fire` frame in #1920's envelope over a WebSocket and records `event.timeStamp`, `performance.now()` at the handler, after the class change and after `send()`, then in the next `requestAnimationFrame` and in a `MessageChannel` task after it (the approximate commit, the method #1924 used); `pad_chain.py` runs an aiohttp 3.14.3 service on 127.0.0.1:8912 that stamps each `fire` and forwards it to an adapter written as #1928 prescribes, a `websockets` 17.1 client on its own daemon thread with its own loop, which stamps arrival; Playwright 1.62.0 drives headless Chromium 151.0.7922.34 (with `has_touch`, `page.touchscreen.tap`, and with `page.mouse.click`) and Firefox 153.0 (both kinds) at 1920 by 1080, 200 taps at 60 ms spacing per case, after ten warm-up taps and a 40-sample ping/pong clock-offset estimate whose min-RTT sample is kept (offset −0.05 to +0.09 ms across the six Chromium runs, +0.56 to +1.24 ms in Firefox, whose `performance.now()` is coarsened to 1 ms so its cross-clock figures carry about 1 ms of noise and small negative minima).

Runs kept: two full passes (`results/run1_pad_chain_*.json` and `results/pad_chain_*.json`, the second summarised in `results/pad_chain_run2.txt`; run 1 has no text summary and is read from its JSON), a third Chromium touch run with `--disable-features=RafAlignedTouchInputEvents,RafAlignedMouseInputEvents` (`results/pad_chain_chromium_touch_noraf.json`) and a 100-tap control with the same harness and no such flags (`results/pad_chain_chromium_touch_ctl.json`), which the flagged run matched. A follow-up pass drove the same page through four Chromium touch-injection paths and three non-touch ones (`verify/touch_paths.py`, output `verify/touch_paths.txt`). `budget.py` assembles the totals from these and the cited terms (`results/budget.txt`); `revise/budget2.py` recomputes every cell of the table below from the saved rows, adds the run 1 comparison, a stated panel allowance and the off-host sampler variants, and prints `revise/budget2.txt`.

`event.timeStamp` is "the time (in milliseconds) at which the event was created" in the same time base as `performance.now()` (https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Event/timeStamp), so input-to-handler measures the browser's own queueing of a synthetic event, not the panel's. No MIDI port and no audio device was opened; the MIDI and audio terms are the sibling findings' and Subsample's own. Three limits on these figures: they are headless browsers with a software compositor on a workstation, and #1924 records that this "says nothing about a Pi's GPU path", while #1921 recommends a Raspberry Pi 5 as the panel host; every transport figure is loopback, with the wire added from ICMP; and the service was measured serving one client, where #1929 measured its broadcast latency rising to 0.65 to 1.3 ms median and 3.9 to 7.9 ms p99 under a sixteen-client load, which is the upper bound to carry when several panels are open.

Four things this settles. First, the page's own work is nothing: class change and `send()` together cost 0.1 to 0.3 ms, and the `fire` frame is on the wire before the paint. Second, the two loopback hops from page to service to adapter cost 1.0 ms median, 1.2 ms at p99 and 1.4 ms at the maximum on Chromium, the same order #1920 measured for a browser round-trip and #1928 for the adapter link; JSON parse and forward in aiohttp is inside the 0.5 ms service term. Third, the visual acknowledgement is dominated by the wait for the next frame, 0 to 16.7 ms uniformly for mouse input and for Firefox touch, so input-to-commit is one frame at most there. Fourth, Chromium's synthetic touch path adds a frame: the handler ran 0.5 to 18 ms after the event's timestamp in clusters that follow the frame boundary, and always about 16 ms before the next rAF, which means the event was delivered just after a frame's rAF callbacks and its paint waited a whole frame; input-to-commit was 17 to 34 ms, two frames at p95, in all three Chromium touch runs.

The extra frame does not follow the injection method. Disabling `RafAlignedTouchInputEvents` changed nothing (5.1 / 17.9 / 18.2 to the handler, 21.7 / 34.1 / 34.6 to commit), and the follow-up pass found the same two-frame commit on all four touch paths tried — Playwright's `touchscreen.tap` (median commit 21.9), CDP `dispatchTouchEvent` with a 30 ms hold (21.9) and back to back (32.9), and CDP `emulateTouchFromMouseEvent` (26.6) — while mouse input on the same touch-enabled context (9.9), mouse input without `has_touch` (9.6) and a CDP pointer of type `pen` (12.1) all committed inside one frame. So it tracks Chromium's touch pipeline rather than the harness. Chrome's own statement is that discrete events "will be dispatched right away" and only continuous ones are aligned to rAF (https://developer.chrome.com/blog/aligning-input-events), so the extra frame is either the compositor-thread touch path or an artefact of injecting touches into a headless renderer at all; which it is can only be settled with a finger on the panel and is the first hardware-gated item below. If it is real, Chromium costs a pad hit and a cell's echo one frame more than Firefox on the same host, which is 17 ms of both latency and jitter and is material to `ui-host`'s browser choice (#1921).

## The step tap's visual acknowledgement

What the finger sees first is drawn locally. Under #1925's recommended option the cell's face is always the server's value and the local, network-free mark is a dim ring drawn on `pointerdown`; the face follows on `ack`, which #1925 emits the moment the helper applies the intent on the loop, "within a few milliseconds on a LAN", and the ring is only settled later by the per-cycle `cycle` event carrying `built_ver`. The budget below is therefore the ring's: the network, the service and Subsequence are not on it. The face costs the same terms plus one browser-to-service-to-app round trip, about 2 to 3 ms on a wired LAN (#1920, #1925, #1928), which is inside Nielsen's 0.1 s instantaneous threshold and not the constraint.

Against the thresholds: with a one-frame browser the whole path stays under Jota's 64 ms and Deber's 69 ms as long as the panel's own touch-down latency is under about 25 ms, and inside Jota's 40 ms window only when it is under a few milliseconds, which no touchscreen is. With the two-frame Chromium behaviour the path exceeds 69 ms whenever the panel term exceeds about 13 ms. Both allowances shrink by whatever the panel's display input lag turns out to be. So the budget the design should hold is: one browser frame from event to commit (act on `pointerdown`, never `pointerup`, never wait for the `ack`, as #1927 and #1920 already say), and the panel's touch-down latency and display input lag measured before the browser is chosen.

Almost nothing further is available in the page: the paint cannot precede the event, and the frame and the vsync are the display's. The one documented lever is the `desynchronized` context attribute, "a boolean value that hints the user agent to reduce the latency by desynchronizing the canvas paint cycle from the event loop" on `2d`, `webgl` and `webgl2` contexts (https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/HTMLCanvasElement/getContext), which Chrome describes as telling "the underlying system to skip as much compositing as it is able" so that "in some cases, the canvas's underlying buffer is sent directly to the screen's display controller", with tearing on front-buffer devices as the cost and stylus drawing as the target case (https://developer.chrome.com/blog/desynchronized). It is a canvas hint and #1931 draws the cells in the DOM, so taking it would mean moving the acknowledgement onto a canvas layer; the frame it can remove is the compositor frame this table prices at 16.7 ms, and whether it removes anything on the panel host is hardware-gated. A 120 Hz host would halve the rAF wait and the vsync, saving up to 16.7 ms with a one-frame browser and up to 25 ms in the Chromium two-frame case; the iiyama's datasheet gives no vertical refresh figure and the 60 Hz assumed here is a third-party listing's (#1924).

Reading it. On one host with a one-frame browser, a wired LAN hop and `buffer_frames` at 128, the browser's event reaches the DAC in 2 to 19 ms with a median of 6; at 256 it is 5 to 28 with a median of 9; at 512, 10 to 45. The median at 128 and 256 is inside Lester and Boley's "Good" to "Fair" band for drums, and the maximum at 256 is at the edge of the region where Jack et al. found 20 ms rated worse than 10 ms; at 512 the median is "Fair" at best and at 1024 the path is past every musical threshold cited. Where Subsample runs barely matters: a second wired hop for an off-host sampler adds about 1 ms, and network MIDI instead of the adapter link is estimated at the same order (#1939), against an output buffer of 3 to 23 ms per period. Direct MIDI from a co-located service (#1939's second option) would save the 0.5 ms adapter hop and nothing else; the service's own hop is 0.45 ms. Neither is where the time goes.

Finger to sound is that plus the panel's touch-down latency, which is the largest unknown in the whole document. With a 15 ms allowance for it the pad path at 128 frames is 17 to 34 ms with a median of about 21, and at 256 frames 20 to 43 with a median of about 24. At the 35 ms guideline bound the maximum is 52 to 54 ms at 128 frames. The two-frame Chromium behaviour, if a real finger reproduces it, adds a median of 16 ms and up to 17 ms of jitter on top of whichever buffer, which alone is sixteen times Wessel and Wright's 1 ms jitter bound and takes the median at 128 frames to 23 ms.

Jitter deserves its own line. The fixed terms (audio buffer, ALSA hop, dispatch) are stable to well under a millisecond; the variable ones are the panel's report interval (4 to 10 ms at 100 to 250 Hz, unmeasured), the network (0.1 ms p99 spread on loopback, about 1 ms on a wired LAN, tens of ms on Wi-Fi), and the browser — but only on paths that wait for a frame. The pad `fire` does not: it is sent inside the `pointerdown` handler, before any rAF, at a measured 0.0 / 0.6 / 1.2 ms from the event timestamp on Chromium mouse and 0 / 0 / 4 on Firefox touch. The browser's frame becomes a pad jitter source only through Chromium's touch dispatch, which is the disputed term above.

A fixed-offset pad path was weighed and is not recommended for v1. Holding each hit to a constant deadline is available: the browser already carries the input timestamp, the transport already estimates the clock offset every 2 s from its ping/pong (#1920, used for the same purpose by #1924), and the delay would be a timer on the adapter's link thread before it sends the note. Subsequence does the same thing internally for a different reason — `_send_offset_seconds` is "``(max_latency − device_latency) / 1000``, clamped ≥ 0. The slowest device returns 0 (sent at logical time); faster devices return a positive delay so they sound together", applied by `_dispatch_with_compensation` (`sequencer.py:1948-1992`), so latency compensation there is deferral of the fast, not early sending, and the same trade is technically open here. What decides against it is the size of what would be traded: on the recommended configuration (wired, one-frame browser) the jitter to remove is about 1 ms of browser, about 1 ms of network and the panel's unmeasured report interval, and buying that with a constant delay large enough to cover the worst case would roughly double a 6 ms median. The trade only becomes attractive if the panel confirms Chromium's two-frame dispatch (17 ms of jitter) or if the panel host is on Wi-Fi (tens of ms), and in the Wi-Fi case the constant would have to be so large that the pad page is better refused than de-jittered. It is deferred, not refused, and it needs no new engine work if it is ever wanted. A pad hit is not compensated against the sequencer's own device latencies either, and should not be: the human's hit time is its own reference.

## The sequencer step tap, for completeness

Its audible latency is the cycle by design: with the default one-beat lookahead a tap lands after one to five beats (500 to 2500 ms at 120 BPM) depending on when in the cycle it is applied, and after one pulse to one cycle plus a pulse with the one-pulse lookahead #1915 recommends (21 to 2021 ms at 120 BPM); the rebuild costs about 0.1 ms per 16 by 8 grid. The write path adds nothing measurable: the adapter queues the set and the sequencer drains it at `reschedule_pulse` and `beat` at a measured p99 of 0.002 ms on the clock (#1928, #1926), and the co-located service leaves Subsequence's median and mean jitter unchanged unless it shares a core (#1929). Two confirmations follow, at very different distances: the `ack` and `changed` leave at application on the loop, a few milliseconds after the tap on a LAN, and turn the ring bright and the face into the server's value; the per-cycle `cycle` event carrying `built_ver` arrives up to one cycle later and is what settles the ring, which is why the ring's lifetime is proportional to where in the cycle the tap fell (#1925). The playhead's error budget is #1924's: 5 to 24 ms behind the MIDI send on a wired LAN with the 60 Hz frame the largest term.

## Substation band switching

From #1938 and #1939: the recommended switch (stop the running `RadioScanner` on its own sentinel, construct a fresh one in the same process) measured 37 to 41 ms from request to the new band's first processed slice with the package's file device on the workstation, then the ten-slice noise-floor warm-up, 2 s on a live device; a process restart adds about 0.75 s of imports and drops the adapter link; on an RTL-SDR with `calibration_frequency_hz` set, both routes add about 13.7 s of calibration; the in-place retune that would avoid the reopen and calibration is deferred. Against Nielsen's limits, a 2 s switch is past the 1 s flow limit and needs the `select` to show pending at once and `changed` when the first slice lands; a 16 s calibrated switch is past the 10 s attention limit and needs progress, which `noise_floor` with `warmup_complete` supplies at the end and nothing supplies during calibration; disabling calibration in a panel-switched deployment, as #1939 suggests, brings the switch back to about 2 s plus the device reopen. Device reopen cost and the whole switch on real hardware are hardware-gated in #1938.

## What is and is not practical over a WAN

From #1920's measurements: a WebSocket round-trip of 15 ms median to a nearby endpoint and about 100 ms to a distant one, on a 5 to 25 ms last mile, with a home Wi-Fi tail of 50 to 80 ms when the channel fluctuates. The grid is unaffected because its local ring is drawn without the network and its audible effect is the cycle; the `ack`, `changed` and `cycle` frames arrive 15 to 100 ms later than on the LAN, which the ring absorbs. The playhead is unaffected in position because #1924 anchors it to timestamps rather than arrival, though a Wi-Fi tail can make the sounding flash miss a cycle's first steps. A pad hit gains 7 to 50 ms one way plus the Wi-Fi tail, so its total is 30 to 100 ms or more with jitter of tens of milliseconds, past every musical threshold above, and the specification already accepts that pads on such a network are not practical; the unreliable side-channel #1920 deferred would trim TCP's head-of-line stalls under loss but not the path. Substation and Subsample's waveforms, lists and faders are unaffected at these scales.

## Short list: how tight to set the pad budget for v1

The grid, the playhead and the band switch have one defensible budget each and no alternative worth tabulating: the grid's acknowledgement is local and frame-bound, the playhead is #1924's, the band switch is what the hardware costs. The pad budget is a genuine choice, and it is one of the decisions the specification's assembly step must put to Simon.

| Option | What v1 promises | What it costs | What it needs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Tight.** `buffer_frames` 128 on the sampler host, wired Ethernet only for the panel, and the browser chosen on latency grounds if the panel reproduces Chromium's two-frame touch | browser event to sound 2 to 19 ms, median 6; finger to sound about 21 ms median on a 15 ms panel allowance | the README's own advice is to lower `buffer_frames` "until you see" xrun warnings "then step back up", so 128 is at the edge and an xrun is audible as clicks; it may reopen `ui-host`'s Chromium kiosk recommendation (#1921) | the panel's touch-down latency measured, the startup `PortAudio output latency` line read on the sampler host, and a browser touch check on the panel |
| **B. Moderate (recommended).** `buffer_frames` 256, wired Ethernet for pads, either browser | browser event to sound 5 to 28 ms, median 9; finger to sound about 24 ms median, 43 ms maximum on the same allowance | about 3 ms of median and 9 ms of worst case against A; the median sits in Lester and Boley's "Fair" band rather than their "Good" one | the same measurements, but the conclusion moves less when they arrive |
| **C. Defer.** No pad budget in v1: the pad page ships unbudgeted, or not at all until the panel is measured, and the page shows its measured round-trip | nothing promised for pads; the grid, the playhead and the display pages are unaffected and keep their budgets | the pad page is the one place the product competes with hardware, and deferring it defers the question rather than answering it | nothing |

**Recommendation: B, with A as the target once the panel's touch-down latency is measured.** The buffer is the largest term the design controls and 256 frames leaves the xrun edge to the sampler host's owner rather than making it a condition of the product; the 3 ms it gives up is inside the noise of the unmeasured panel term, so tightening to A is a change to make when there is a measurement to justify it rather than now. C's round-trip readout should be adopted whichever option is chosen: it costs a line on the page and it is the only way a player sees that the network has taken the pads out of budget. Every option is conditional on the same measurement, which is why the panel's touch-down latency heads the hardware-gated list.

## Hardware-gated

- The panel's touch report rate and touch-down latency, the largest unmeasured term in both budgets: `evtest` timestamps for the rate (already listed in #1917), and for the latency the acoustic method Microsoft's own kit specifies — an acoustic sensor on the glass and a finger tap, the latency being "the difference between the sound wave that is created by the touch that is reported through the microcontroller, and the touch event" seen by the OS (https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/windows/hardware/hck/dn195876(v=vs.85), the method page that https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-hardware/design/component-guidelines/touch-response-latency--down- defers to). Any recording that captures the tap and the response in one audio file gives the same difference: with `page.html` playing a click, or with the drum machine's or sampler's output in the recording, one file gives finger-to-sound end to end.
- The panel's display input lag (signal to pixel), which the tables above do not carry and which comes off the touch-down allowance: a photodiode or a high-speed camera on the lit cell, as #1924 lists. The 5 ms figure in the tables is pixel response only.
- Whether real touch on the panel host shows Chromium's two-frame dispatch: load `page.html` from any server on the panel host's browsers (Chromium kiosk on the Pi 5, Edge on Windows, Firefox) and read `window.records`; the harness's own numbers say what one frame and two look like, and `verify/touch_paths.txt` says what every synthetic path looks like.
- The rAF cadence, the commit-to-glass depth and the refresh rate driven on the panel host, as #1924 lists; every browser figure here is headless on a workstation with a software compositor.
- `PortAudio output latency` as printed on the sampler host, and `scripts/measure_midi_latency.py` and `scripts/measure_handler_timing.py` there; then the finger-to-sound recording above with the real service, adapter and Subsample.
- Browser-to-service round-trip from the panel host across the real wired and Wi-Fi segments (#1920's one-liner). This needs only a second host that may run Python, not the panel.
- The band switch on the live RTL-SDR, HackRF and AirSpy (#1938).

## Not covered

- The panel's own input latency and report rate: no figure exists in iiyama's datasheet (#1917) and the panel is not attached to this workstation; the Windows guideline bound is used in the tables and marked as such, and a 15 ms allowance is used where a single figure is needed, which is an assumption and not a measurement.
- A browser-to-service round-trip across a real LAN segment: every transport figure here is loopback and the wire term is ICMP, re-measured on this LAN in this pass (0.35 to 0.52 ms round-trip to a wired neighbour, 1.63 to 3.77 ms to the router) but still not a browser measurement. #1920 deferred the same run for the same reason: no second host on which this session was entitled to run Python.
- Whether the Chromium touch dispatch delay is real or an artefact of synthetic injection: four injection paths agree, which rules out the harness's choice of API but not headless injection as a whole; a physical touch is the only test.
- The drum machine's and synth's own MIDI-in-to-sound latency, which lags the realised sound behind the MIDI send on the grid path: it is rig-specific, #1912 puts Simon's particular instruments out of scope, and #1924 records the same gap for the playhead.
- The primary texts of Jack et al. 2016 and 2018 are paywalled from this workstation, so the percussion figures are taken from Schmid et al. 2024, whose full text was read, and the two papers are cited jointly as Schmid cites them.
- PortAudio's period count on the sampler host, which sets the multiple between the README's period table and the real output latency; the startup line is the ground truth and it is hardware-gated. The one-to-three-period model used in the tables is the README's "small multiple" made numeric, not a measurement.
- No end-to-end measurement joins the browser to Subsample's callback here: the adapter in the harness stops at receipt, and the 0.235 ms MIDI hop is #1937's, measured against a probe port rather than `MidiPlayer`.
- The service's own cost under several panels: the figures here are one client, and #1929's sixteen-client broadcast latency is carried as the upper bound rather than re-measured.
